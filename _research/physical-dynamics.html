Generating Physical Dynamics under Priors

01 How can we tell whether generated motion obeys physics?

Particle trajectories can be continuous and smooth, and flow fields can display familiar textures. Yet these visual features do not guarantee conservation of energy or momentum, or compliance with the equations governing a system's evolution.

  1. Input: data and known priorsTraining trajectories or physical fields, together with symmetries, conservation laws, or known equations.
  2. Learning: a distribution of dynamicsDiffusion training learns the joint variation across an entire trajectory or field.
  3. Output: new physical samplesGenerate particle positions and velocities, or physical fields that vary over space and time.

The goal here is to learn a sample distribution, rather than simply fit a particular future value. The three-body system generates three-dimensional motion of three particles unconditionally; the five-spring system generates two-dimensional motion conditioned on the graph of spring connections between particles; the PDE tasks include conditional prediction and physical-field generation.

Distributional priors: which changes preserve the physics?

Rotating an entire system, changing the coordinate origin, or relabeling interchangeable particles changes the representation of a sample. The model should respond consistently to these transformations, making use of the data's inherent symmetries.

Feasibility priors: which samples violate physics?

A trajectory's total momentum and total energy should remain constant as specified by the system; a physical field should satisfy its governing equation. These requirements can be measured through residuals and incorporated into training.

The central question

How can we give a diffusion model both the symmetries of a distribution and the equations its samples must satisfy, while applying constraints to the right prediction target?

From whole samples to denoising predictions

02 What exactly does a diffusion model predict?

Let \(x_0\) denote an entire clean trajectory or physical field. Diffusion time \(t\) controls the noise level; it is a different dimension from the physical time within a trajectory.

\[x_t=\alpha_t x_0+\sigma_t\epsilon,\qquad \epsilon\sim\mathcal N(0,I).\]

During training, noise is added to an entire sample, and the model learns to recover the clean data or predict the added noise. During generation, it starts from Gaussian noise and follows the reverse diffusion process to produce a new trajectory or field step by step; each step does not merely predict the next physical time step.

Two training objectives connected to the same score

\[\begin{aligned} \mathcal L_{\mathrm{noise}}&=\mathbb E\!\left[w(t)\|\epsilon_\theta(x_t,t)-\epsilon\|^2\right],\\ \mathcal L_{\mathrm{data}}&=\mathbb E\!\left[w(t)\|x_\theta(x_t,t)-x_0\|^2\right]. \end{aligned}\]

At the ideal squared-error optima, noise prediction and data prediction correspond to:

\[\epsilon_\theta^*(x_t,t)=-\sigma_t\nabla_{x_t}\log q_t(x_t),\qquad x_\theta^*(x_t,t)=\mathbb E[x_0\mid x_t].\]

The score \(\nabla_{x_t}\log q_t(x_t)\) describes the local direction of change in the current noisy distribution. A noise prediction can also be converted into a clean-data estimate:

\[\hat x_0(x_t,t)=\frac{x_t-\sigma_t\epsilon_\theta(x_t,t)}{\alpha_t}.\]
The starting point for constraint design

Ideally, the denoising prediction is the conditional mean \(\mathbb E[x_0\mid x_t]\). It is not the same as any particular true clean sample.

Distributional priors

03 Keep coordinate transformations and model predictions consistent

Physical content can be separated from its representation: observing the same particle motion from a different direction still describes the same system.

Three questions connect distributional symmetry to model design: does noising preserve the symmetries of clean data, how should the score transform, and how do predictions at one representative of each equivalence class extend to the whole class?

Represent global translation classes with centered coordinates

For the theoretical analysis, global translation can be removed by working in a centered Euclidean subspace \(E\). Subtracting one mean center, computed over all positions in the entire trajectory, preserves the center's motion over physical time. The same displacement is subtracted at every frame, and velocities remain unchanged.

Densities and gradients are defined on the modeled space \(E\), where the following theorems describe rotations and particle permutations. Noise also lies in \(E\): orthogonally projecting a standard Gaussian onto \(E\) produces isotropic Gaussian noise on that subspace.

Theorem 1 · Isotropic noising preserves orthogonal symmetries

Assumptions. Let \(E\) be a finite-dimensional Euclidean space on which a group \(\mathcal G\) acts through orthogonal operators \(Q\). Suppose the clean random variable satisfies \(QX_0\overset{d}{=}X_0\) for every \(Q\in\mathcal G\), and let

\[ X_t=\alpha_tX_0+\sigma_t\epsilon,\qquad \epsilon\sim\mathcal N_E(0,I_E),\quad \epsilon\perp\!\!\!\perp X_0,\quad \sigma_t>0. \]

Here \(\alpha_t\) is a scalar, \(I_E\) is the identity on this space, and \(\overset{d}{=}\) denotes equality in distribution. Densities are defined relative to volume measure on \(E\); “ambient score” below likewise means the gradient in the modeled space \(E\). Rotations, particle permutations, and their compositions can all be represented by such orthogonal actions.

Conclusion. \(X_t\) has a positive smooth density \(q_t\) on \(E\), and its density and score satisfy

\[ q_t(Qx)=q_t(x),\qquad s_t(Qx)=Q\,s_t(x),\qquad s_t=\nabla_E\log q_t. \]

Proof. Isotropic Gaussian noise satisfies \(Q\epsilon\overset{d}{=}\epsilon\). Together with independence, this gives

\[ QX_t=\alpha_tQX_0+\sigma_tQ\epsilon \overset{d}{=}\alpha_tX_0+\sigma_t\epsilon=X_t. \]

Orthogonal transformations preserve volume, so equality in distribution implies equality of densities. Differentiating \(\log q_t(Qx)=\log q_t(x)\) then gives \(Q^\top s_t(Qx)=s_t(x)\), establishing score equivariance.

Intuitively, rotating the input should rotate its denoising direction; relabeling particles should reorder the output in the same way. In a joint position–velocity representation, rotation acts on both components. Conditional generation also requires transforming the condition: the five-spring connection graph must follow the particle permutation. With a condition held fixed, only symmetries preserving that condition can be required.

From group orbits to representatives: the ECM intuition

Samples related by symmetry transformations belong to the same orbit:

\[[x]=\{Qx:Q\in\mathcal G\}.\]

Choosing one representative per orbit describes the same intrinsic structures with fewer representations. Here ECM denotes this set of representatives. The model predicts the ambient score evaluated at each representative, with the gradient taken in the modeled space \(E\). A measurable choice of representatives is sufficient for the result below; a globally smooth selection map is not required.

Theorem 2 · Extending an equivariant score from orbit representatives

Assumptions. Use the same orthogonal group action and fix \(t\). Let \(q_t\) be a positive \(C^1\) density on \(E\) with \(q_t(Qx)=q_t(x)\). Let a measurable map \(\varphi\) choose the same representative for every point on an orbit, with \(\varphi(x)\in[x]\), and let \(\nu_t\) be the distribution of \(\varphi(X_t)\). Suppose the model is exactly equivariant:

\[s_\theta(Qx,t)=Q\,s_\theta(x,t)\qquad(Q\in\mathcal G),\]

For \(\nu_t\)-almost every representative \(z\), suppose it predicts the ambient score:

\[ s_\theta(z,t)=s_t(z) =\left.\nabla_E\log q_t(x)\right|_{x=z}. \]

Conclusion. \(s_\theta(x,t)=s_t(x)\) for \(q_t\)-almost every \(x\). More generally, the prediction error has exactly the same length along an orbit:

\[ \|s_\theta(x,t)-s_t(x)\|^2 =\|s_\theta(\varphi(x),t)-s_t(\varphi(x))\|^2. \]

Proof. Set \(z=\varphi(x)\) and choose a group action with \(x=Qz\). Equivariance of both the model and the true score gives

\[ s_\theta(x,t)-s_t(x) =Q\bigl(s_\theta(z,t)-s_t(z)\bigr). \]

Orthogonal transformations preserve vector lengths, so zero error at a representative gives zero error on its whole orbit. “Almost every representative” is measured by \(\nu_t\) and transfers to the ambient space through the law of \(\varphi(X_t)\).

Predictions at representatives determine the whole orbit

An exactly equivariant model extends ambient score predictions at representatives to every symmetry-related representation. ECM provides a theoretical basis for sharing information across representations of the same structure. The project uses training objectives and symmetry augmentation to exploit this structure, while generation follows reverse diffusion.

Equivariance of noise and data predictions

Under the orthogonal actions in Theorem 1, the noise predictor \(\epsilon^*=-\sigma_t s_t\) inherits score equivariance. For \(\alpha_t\ne0\), the optimal data predictor also satisfies

\[ D_t^*(x)=\frac{x+\sigma_t^2s_t(x)}{\alpha_t}, \qquad D_t^*(Qx)=Q\,D_t^*(x). \]

Both optimal predictors therefore respect rotation and permutation symmetries. The choice between noise matching and data matching depends on the structure of the data and empirical performance.

How do theoretical requirements become training choices?

These design choices reflect the structure of the data. Data augmentation helps a model learn symmetries, but it does not provide a strict architectural guarantee of equivariance for every input.

The Jensen gap

04 If every sample is feasible, why can their mean violate physics?

Suppose clean samples satisfy a physical residual \(\mathcal R(x_0)=0\). Can we directly require the denoising prediction to satisfy the same equation? The answer depends on the form of the constraint.

\[\mathcal R\!\left(\mathbb E[x_0\mid x_t]\right)\ne\mathbb E[\mathcal R(x_0)\mid x_t]=0.\]

Nonlinear transformations generally do not commute with expectation: this is the Jensen gap discussed here. At high noise levels, the same \(x_t\) can correspond to multiple plausible clean samples; averaging these possibilities can produce a state outside the feasible set.

Linear momentum constraints commute with expectation

Total momentum is a linear combination of velocities. For fixed masses, the weighted sum commutes with conditional expectation, so a linear residual can act directly on the denoised estimate.

Nonlinear energy constraints need special treatment

Kinetic energy contains squared velocities, and potential energy involves distances between particles. In general, \((\mathbb E[V\mid x_t])^2\ne\mathbb E[V^2\mid x_t]\), so directly penalizing the total energy of a predicted state may conflict with the correct denoising target.

The challenge goes beyond adding a physics loss

We must also ask whether that physics loss is suitable for the statistic the model currently predicts.

Physical feasibility priors

05 Choose supervision to match the constraint's structure

Retain the diffusion objective and add training terms that reflect the physical structure. \(\lambda\) controls the relative strength of data fitting and physical supervision.

\[\mathcal L(\theta)=\mathcal L_{\mathrm{diffusion}}(\theta)+\lambda\,\mathcal L_{\mathrm{physics}}(\theta).\]

01 / Linear and affine Constrain the denoised output directly

If \(\mathcal R(x)=Ax+b\), expectation commutes with the constraint and the Jensen gap is zero. We can use:

\[\mathcal L_{\mathrm{physics}}=\mathbb E\!\left[w(t)\|\mathcal R(\hat x_0(x_t,t))\|^2\right].\]

Total momentum and linear PDE residuals with fixed coefficients after finite-difference discretization follow this idea. It removes the conflict in the form of the objectives; with finite data and model capacity, the residual may still be nonzero.

02 / Multilinear Fix other variables and supervise a linear component

Split a sample into \(x_0=(u_0,v_0)\). If fixing \(v_0\) makes the constraint linear in \(u_0\), we can use coefficients from the true sample during training:

\[\mathcal R(x_0)=W(v_0)u_0+b(v_0),\qquad \mathcal L_{\mathrm{physics}}=\mathbb E\!\left[w(t)\|W(v_0)u_\theta(x_t,t)+b(v_0)\|^2\right].\]

When the true sample has zero residual, this expression is a reconstruction error weighted by \(W^\top W\). The key is to fix the other ground-truth variables during training, rather than replace every variable with a predicted mean. When the weights depend on the sample, it also cannot simply be assumed to have exactly the same optimum as ordinary mean squared error.

03 / Convex constraints Account for approximation error

For a scalar convex function satisfying the required conditions, Jensen's inequality gives:

\[\mathcal R\!\left(\mathbb E[x_0\mid x_t]\right)\leq\mathbb E[\mathcal R(x_0)\mid x_t].\]

This inequality does not imply that the constraint equality holds at the mean. The method uses the convex case for an approximate treatment; under the relevant regularity and posterior concentration conditions, a more concentrated conditional distribution gives a smaller Jensen gap. This informs training design but provides no exact guarantee for arbitrary nonlinear constraints.

04 / General nonlinear constraints Change the quantity being predicted

If the constraint can be written as \(\mathcal R(x)=g(h(x))\), it can be decomposed into simpler components; alternatively, an auxiliary output head sharing the backbone representation can specifically predict \(h(x_0)\):

\[\mathcal L_h=\mathbb E\!\left[w(t)\|\tilde h_\theta(x_t,t)-h(x_0)\|^2\right].\]

For example, an auxiliary head can learn squared velocities or distance-related quantities, giving the shared representation energy-related supervision. This is a different task from predicting a mean velocity and then squaring it. Auxiliary supervision helps the main model learn physical structure, but its effect must still be verified on the final generated samples.

From conservation laws to training terms

06 Why do momentum and energy need different treatments?

Particle samples contain both positions \(C\) and velocities \(V\). Let \(\ell\) denote physical time within a trajectory and \(t\) denote diffusion noise time.

Momentum: penalize variation over time within a trajectory

\[P_\ell=\sum_{k=1}^{K}m_kV_{\ell,k},\qquad \bar P=\frac1L\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}P_\ell.\]
\[\mathcal L_P=\mathbb E\!\left[w(t)\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\|\hat P_\ell-\bar{\hat P}\|^2\right].\]

The constraint requires total momentum to remain constant within a trajectory, without requiring all samples to share the same momentum value. This residual is linear in velocity, making it suitable for the denoised output directly; the main five-spring experiment uses this type of training term.

Three-body: from energy conservation to a loss

Consider three equal masses \(m\), counting each pair once. Follow conservation → energy features → component errors → training loss.

01 · Start with conservation
\[E_\ell=\underbrace{-Gm^2\sum_{i<j}\frac1{r_{\ell,ij}}}_{U_\ell}+\underbrace{\frac m2\sum_{k,d}V_{\ell,k,d}^{\,2}}_{K_\ell}=E^*.\]

Potential energy \(U_\ell\) and kinetic energy \(K_\ell\) can exchange; only their sum stays constant over physical time \(\ell\). Each trajectory may have a different \(E^*\).

↓
02 · Extract nonlinear features; the outer sum becomes linear
\[u_{\ell,ij}=\frac1{r_{\ell,ij}},\qquad s_{\ell,k,d}=V_{\ell,k,d}^{\,2}\] \[E_\ell=-Gm^2\sum_{i<j}u_{\ell,ij}+\frac m2\sum_{k,d}s_{\ell,k,d}=a^\top h_\ell.\]

Stack all \(u,s\) into \(h_\ell\), with coefficients in \(a\); distances are \(r_{\ell,ij}=\|C_{\ell,i}-C_{\ell,j}\|\).

↓
03 · Why supervise the features?
\[\hat E_\ell-E^*=a^\top(\hat h_\ell-h_\ell)\] \[|\hat E_\ell-E^*|^2\leq\|a\|^2\,\|\hat h_\ell-h_\ell\|^2.\]

Hats denote denoised predictions. By Cauchy–Schwarz, feature error bounds energy error; component losses penalize kinetic and potential errors even when they cancel in total energy.

↓

04 · Match the clean trajectory frame by frame; square and sum each error

Inverse-distance error
\[\mathcal L_r=\sum_{\ell,i<j}(\hat u_{\ell,ij}-u_{\ell,ij})^2\] \[=\sum_{\ell,i<j}\left(\frac1{\hat r_{\ell,ij}}-\frac1{r_{\ell,ij}}\right)^2\]
Squared-velocity component error
\[\mathcal L_v=\sum_{\ell,k,d}(\hat s_{\ell,k,d}-s_{\ell,k,d})^2\] \[=\sum_{\ell,k,d}\left(\hat V_{\ell,k,d}^{\,2}-V_{\ell,k,d}^{\,2}\right)^2\]
↓
Weight the terms and add the diffusion loss
\[\mathcal L_E=\mathbb E_{x_0,t,\epsilon}\!\left[w_1(t)\mathcal L_r+w_2(t)\mathcal L_v\right]\] \[\mathcal L=\mathcal L_{\mathrm{noise}}+\mathcal L_E.\]

The main experiment computes \(\hat u=1/\hat r\) and \(\hat s=\hat V^2\) directly from denoised positions and velocities, without specifying \(E^*\).

This is soft component supervision motivated by conservation, not an equivalent rewriting of it. It neither requires kinetic and potential energy to remain separately constant nor guarantees exact conservation in generated samples. Duplicate-pair factors can be absorbed into \(w_1\).

What do the specific networks handle?

The three-body model uses a GRU for time series; the five-spring model uses an EGNN to extract particle and edge features, combined with a GRU for temporal variation; the PDE models use a GRU or U-Net according to the task, with U-Net variants for Darcy, Burgers, and shallow water. The framework focuses on matching output targets with physical losses, rather than prescribing a single network for every physical system.

Why the design matters

08 Where do the improvements come from?

Directly adding a nonlinear energy constraint may not help

The three-body ablation compares three training settings: no constraint, directly penalizing variation in the denoised output's total energy, and decomposing the constraint into energy-related quantities. This ablation is a separate comparison from the main experiment.

Symmetry augmentation and representation capacity also affect results

Permutation augmentation must reorder both particles and connections. It improves several errors, but not every metric decreases. The nonlinear energy variant for the five-spring system also requires enough information to predict inter-particle distances: when a GRU alone provides an insufficient representation, the reducible treatment is not applicable; auxiliary prediction using EGNN edge features is more effective.

How physical knowledge enters a generative model

09 Understand the prediction target before imposing constraints

The key to this project is aligning physical priors with the statistical targets of diffusion models.

  1. Organize distributions through symmetryHandle rotations, translations, and particle permutations consistently, and choose a matching objective suited to the data structure.
  2. Understand constraints through the conditional meanLinear residuals can act directly; nonlinear residuals require accounting for the Jensen gap or changing the supervised quantity.
  3. Test physics on generated samplesEvaluate dynamics errors together with conservation or equation residuals, distinguishing visual examples from aggregate metrics.

What do these results support?

The experiments show that appropriate physical training terms can reduce feasibility errors across several systems and may also improve dynamics quality. The gains depend on the system, constraint form, representation capacity, and training settings; improvements in five-spring dynamic error and PDE residuals are relatively limited.

Soft constraints and energy-related supervision during training do not guarantee that every generated sample exactly satisfies conservation laws. Finite-difference residuals also depend on the discretization grid; physical fields may still contain noise and errors in high-frequency details. The model also requires usable priors or computable related quantities, so this method should not be understood as solving arbitrary unknown equations without physical knowledge.

The main point to take away

In a generative model, an appropriate physical constraint must describe the real world and match the object the model is estimating.
