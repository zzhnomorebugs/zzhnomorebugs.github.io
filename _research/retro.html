Single-step retrosynthesis

01 Given a target molecule, how can its reactants be found?

Single-step retrosynthesis predicts possible sets of reactants from a target product. It is a fundamental component of multistep synthesis planning.

  1. Input: product molecular graphAtoms are nodes, and chemical bonds are edges.
  2. Prediction: where changes occurIdentify possible reaction centers and structural transformations.
  3. Output: reactant candidatesAllow multiple possible routes to synthesize the product.

This task involves more than breaking a bond. The model must also change atom attributes, recover atoms absent from the product, and connect them into plausible reactants. Meanwhile, much of the molecular scaffold often remains unchanged.

The question this project addresses

Can a generative model use the structural prior of “where the reaction occurs” to learn more efficiently “what the reactants should look like”?

Task definition and evaluation

Given the product graph \(G_P\), learn the conditional distribution \(p(G_R\mid G_P)\). Here, \(G_R\) is the graph representing the reactant set and need not have only one connected component.

The primary metric is Top-\(k\) Exact Match: whether the reactant set recorded in the dataset appears among the top \(k\) predictions. It measures agreement with the recorded answer; a mismatch does not necessarily mean that another synthesis route is chemically infeasible. This page focuses on single-step prediction and does not directly address full multistep route search.

Motivation

02 Preserve both structural priors and generative flexibility

Retrosynthesis naturally admits a useful task decomposition: first locate the reaction center, then generate the corresponding reactant structures.

Table 1 · Comparison of retrosynthesis approaches
ApproachHow it worksMain trade-off
Template-basedMatch transformations from a reaction template libraryExplicit rules, but limited by template coverage
Semi-template-basedLocate the reaction center, then complete intermediate fragmentsUses task decomposition, but some methods still rely on completion rules
Template-freeDirectly generate reactant sequences or graphsFlexible generation, but reaction sites and structural changes must be learned from data

RETRO draws on reaction-center localization and supplies it as conditioning information to a template-free generator. The key is to express reaction sites through the atom ordering of a molecular graph, allowing general-purpose positional encoding to carry reaction-center information.

Key insight: the same molecule can have different atom orderings

The graph has the same chemical connectivity, yet its nodes can enter the model in different orders. If the sequence always begins at a reaction center, the model gains a stable reference position.

The prior here is a reaction-center-related positional pattern; the generative model still predicts the specific atoms, bonds, and attributes of the reactants.

Reaction sites → atom ordering

03 Let the beginning of the sequence point to the reaction center

Choose a reaction-center atom as the root, order the product atoms deterministically, and append placeholder nodes for generating additional atoms.

  1. Head · Reaction-center rootSpecify the current candidate reaction site.
  2. Body · Product scaffoldRetain an atom ordering consistent with that root.
  3. Tail · Dummy nodesReserve space for additional atoms in the reactants.

During training, atom mapping between products and reactants identifies the ground-truth reaction centers, and training examples are constructed with different center atoms as roots. At inference, only the product is available, so a separate graph neural network first predicts candidate centers. The atoms are then ordered from each candidate root to generate reactants.

How ordering is implemented and reaction centers are defined

The implementation uses RDKit's rooted canonical SMILES to determine node order: after setting rootedAtAtom, it reads the atom output order and consistently reorders nodes and edges. SMILES determines the permutation here; the object being generated remains a molecular graph.

The reaction center includes atoms involved in bond formation, bond breaking, or bond-order changes, as well as atoms with changes in attributes such as charge, hydrogen count, or chirality. For multiple center atoms in one example, a separate ordering can be constructed with each as the root.

Sequence order relates to the structural environment of the reaction center, but sequence distance is not the same as shortest-path distance on the graph. Rooting at one center also does not imply that all center atoms must appear consecutively at the beginning.

RetroDiT

04 Use positional encoding to make the ordering readable

Atom ordering carries the reaction prior. RetroDiT introduces relative sequence positions into attention through RoPE and jointly updates nodes and edges.

Model atoms and bonds together

The node branch handles atom attributes, while the edge branch handles bond information. Edge features modulate attention between nodes, and interaction information from attention also contributes to edge updates.

RoPE provides a relative positional reference

With the reaction center fixed at the beginning of the sequence, positional encoding helps the model distinguish the root, scaffold, and trailing placeholder region, and learn relationships among them.

Time conditioning specifies the generation stage

The model receives both the current time and the intermediate molecular graph, then predicts the final reactants' node and edge category distributions.

How does RoPE enter attention?

\[(R_iq_i)^\top(R_jk_j)=q_i^\top R_{j-i}k_j.\]

Position-dependent rotations act on queries and keys, making attention scores depend on the relative index \(j-i\). Ordering supplies a semantic reference, and RoPE lets the model use it. Changing the permutation alone, without letting the model perceive positions, cannot fully realize this design's benefit.

Discrete flow matching

05 Generate reactants step by step from the product graph

Discrete flow matching directly connects two structured states: the product and the reactants. Training learns to predict the endpoint; inference uses the predicted distributions to construct transitions between discrete states.

  1. Construct an intermediate stateSample between product and reactant node and edge categories.
  2. Learn to predict the endpointRetroDiT predicts reactants from the intermediate graph and time.
  3. Sample along the trajectoryStart from the product and progressively update atoms and bonds.

Training need not simulate the full generation trajectory. Each iteration directly samples a time and an intermediate state, supervising endpoint prediction with cross-entropy over node and edge categories. At inference, graph transformation is completed by discretized sampling of a continuous-time Markov chain.

20–50Sampling steps

These achieve good generation performance in the reported experiments; the main experiments use 50 steps.

The RetroBridge setting. A 10–25-fold reduction in steps does not imply the same reduction in end-to-end runtime.

Training objective, sampling curves, and efficiency measures

During training, atom mapping keeps product and reactant node indices aligned, with additional atoms assigned to the trailing placeholder nodes.

For a discrete node or edge variable \(z\), the product state \(z_0\) and reactant state \(z_1\) define the interpolation:

\[p_t(z\mid z_0,z_1)=(1-t)\delta_{z_0}(z)+t\delta_{z_1}(z).\]

Nodes and edges are sampled in this way, so an intermediate graph can contain local states from both the product and reactants. The training objective can be summarized as:

\[\mathcal L=\mathbb E_{t,G_0,G_1,G_t}\!\left[\operatorname{CE}\!\left(G_1,p^\theta_{1\mid t}(\cdot\mid G_t)\right)\right].\]

The training cost comparison is: RetroBridge, about 72 hours on one GPU, totaling 72 GPU-hours; this method, about 1.5 hours on 8 GPUs, totaling 12 GPU-hours.

To what extent can structural guidance substitute for model scale?

The most striking comparison is that a 280K-parameter model with Oracle-center guidance reaches about 51% Top-1, comparable to the 65M model with Canonical ordering (50.4%). This shows that an accurate structural prior can substantially reduce the learning burden; the conclusion depends on access to ground-truth reaction centers.

A candidate-generation case and evaluation details

This case shows that the same chemical structure can produce different candidate distributions under different input permutations. It illustrates how ordering works; overall gains should be assessed through dataset experiments and ablations.

Evaluation draws 100 samples for each product, standardizes and deduplicates the generated structures, ranks them by frequency, and then computes Top-\(k\). Values in the main table and scaling ablations differ slightly; this page cites each from its corresponding experiment.

What remains

07 The current bottleneck: prior quality determines its benefit

A substantial gap separates Oracle and predicted centers. Reaction centers provide useful guidance, but incorrect predictions can also mislead generation.

This result suggests that future improvements should address both the accuracy and uncertainty of center prediction: improve candidate-center coverage, retain multiple hypotheses when uncertain, and study how the generator can reduce its dependence on incorrect centers.

The central lesson of this project

The representation order of a molecular graph can carry a prior about reaction sites. Reaction-center-rooted ordering connects positional encoding to the chemistry task. Combined with graph generation and discrete flow matching, it forms a retrosynthesis system whose components can be improved separately.

Limitations and further questions

  • Center errors propagate. Experiments with ground-truth centers provide a diagnostic reference; they do not guarantee that an improved predictor will reach the same result.
  • Capacity for additional atoms is limited. The number of Dummy nodes bounds the number of extra reactant atoms that can be accommodated. Structures exceeding the reserved capacity require an extended representation.
  • Benchmark scores are not experimental success rates. Exact Match depends on dataset records; real synthesis also requires reaction conditions, availability, and experimental validation.
  • A gap remains between single-step prediction and multistep planning. Integrating candidate diversity and confidence with route search remains a future question.

These directions are discussions based on the experimental results, not outcomes already validated by this work.
