Geometry before sampling

01 Model internal shape while removing global rotation and translation

A molecule or human skeleton retains the same internal geometry after a global rotation or translation. Distances remove these degrees of freedom, but they also constrain one another: changing an arbitrary edge may leave no realizable three-dimensional structure.

  1. Input: structural conditions and noisy coordinatesA molecular graph or pose category provides the condition; coordinates describe the current three-dimensional state.
  2. Model: invariant changes in distancePairwise distances describe internal geometry. Predict a denoising direction, then convert it into coordinate displacements.
  3. Output: generated 3D coordinatesRecover internal shape through sampling updates that require no explicit manifold projection.

Distances are invariant; coordinate updates must be equivariant

Let \(\mathcal C=[x_1,\ldots,x_n]^\top\in\mathbb R^{n\times3}\), and let \(d\) be the complete pairwise distance matrix. Here, “adjacency matrix” means a weighted distance matrix, rather than a binary matrix of chemical bonds.

\[\begin{aligned}d_{ij}&=\|x_i-x_j\|,\qquad d=\operatorname{adj}(\mathcal C),\\ \mathcal C'&=\mathcal C R^\top+\mathbf1 b^\top,\quad R\in SO(3),\\ \operatorname{adj}(\mathcal C')&=\operatorname{adj}(\mathcal C).\end{aligned}\tag{1}\]

Scalar distance predictions should remain invariant, while coordinate displacements should rotate with the input. This lets the model focus on relative geometry. Translation can be fixed by centering the coordinates, avoiding the interpretation of a “uniform distribution” over unbounded translations as a normalizable coordinate density.

The challenge: realizable distances occupy only part of matrix space

\[M=\left\{d:\exists\mathcal C\in\mathbb R^{n\times3},\ d_{ij}=\|x_i-x_j\|\right\}.\tag{2}\]

For example, the distances among three points must satisfy the triangle inequalities. Realizability in three dimensions imposes stronger constraints, so independently adding Gaussian noise to each edge through \(d+\epsilon\) generally leaves the feasible set \(M\). For nondegenerate structures, the local number of shape degrees of freedom is typically \(3n-6\), far below the \(n(n-1)/2\) edge lengths. Collisions and rank-deficient configurations require separate treatment.

The idea throughout this walkthrough

Add noise to coordinates to obtain distances that remain realizable. Describe denoising in distance space, then transfer that direction back to coordinates with an inexpensive local operator. The approximation concerns this geometric conversion, as well as numerical integration.

Distances are also invariant under reflection. Complete distances generally determine a structure only up to translation and \(O(3)\), so they cannot distinguish mirror images on their own; tasks involving chirality need additional information. The distance representation below is therefore not treated as a global one-to-one parameterization of the entire \(SE(3)\) quotient space.

Forward diffusion and probability flow

02 First understand how SDEs and ODEs denoise in Euclidean space

The score points toward increasing density in the current distribution. A reverse SDE combines deterministic denoising with random perturbations; a probability-flow ODE describes the same evolution of marginal distributions through a deterministic vector field. This correspondence depends on the diffusion coefficient: simply deleting the SDE noise term is insufficient.

\[\mathrm dx_t=f(t)x_t\,\mathrm dt+g(t)\,\mathrm dW_t,\qquad x_0\sim q_0.\tag{3}\]

Forward time runs from \(0\) to \(T\), gradually destroying structure. For state-independent scalar noise, the reverse process and probability flow are:

\[\begin{aligned} \mathrm dx_t&=\left[f(t)x_t-g^2(t)\nabla_x\log q_t(x_t)\right]\mathrm dt+g(t)\,\mathrm d\overline W_t,\\ \frac{\mathrm dx_t}{\mathrm dt}&=f(t)x_t-\frac12g^2(t)\nabla_x\log q_t(x_t). \end{aligned}\tag{4}\]

Generation integrates from \(T\) toward \(0\), with \(\mathrm dt<0\), so the score term with a preceding minus sign actually moves samples toward higher density. The ODE factor \(1/2\) comes from the diffusion term in the Fokker–Planck equation. With an exact score and the other required conditions, the two processes share the distribution at each time; their sample paths need not coincide.

The ODE in this work focuses on variance-exploding (VE) noise

\[\begin{aligned} \mathrm d\mathcal C_t&=\mathbf f(\mathcal C_t,t)\,\mathrm dt+\mathbf G(\mathcal C_t,t)\,\mathrm dW_t,\\ \text{VE:}\quad \mathbf f&=0,\quad \mathbf G=G(t)I,\quad G^2(t)=\frac{\mathrm d\sigma_t^2}{\mathrm dt},\\ \mathcal C_t\mid\mathcal C_0&\sim\mathcal N(\mathcal C_0,\sigma_t^2 I). \end{aligned}\tag{5}\]

The last line takes the initial noise variance to be zero and treats coordinates as a \(3n\)-dimensional vector; centering restricts the process to the corresponding centered subspace. In VE diffusion, the mean stays fixed as the noise scale grows. The explicit distance-drift formulas below use this setting and do not transfer directly to arbitrary noise schedules.

Coordinates, distances, and projection

03 Express a distance update as a coordinate reconstruction problem

Writing the reverse process on the distance manifold means that every step must bring proposed distances back to the feasible set and recover a set of coordinates. Combining these operations reveals where the computational cost arises.

Start with an idealized update that requires projection

Following the paper’s manifold formulation, let \(\mathcal B_t^M\) be manifold Brownian motion. Under suitable geometric, metric, and noise assumptions, distance diffusion is written as follows. This does not mean that arbitrary coordinate noise induces an isotropic distance process.

\[\mathrm dd_t=f(d_t)\,\mathrm dt+g(t)\,\mathrm d\mathcal B_t^M.\tag{6}\]

Let \(t_k=k\tau\). One reverse discrete step proposes distances and projects them as follows:

\[\begin{aligned} F^{\mathcal N}(d_t,t)&=\tau\left[f(d_t)-g^2(t)S_t(d_t)\right]+\sqrt\tau g(t)z^{t,d_t},\\ d^{k-1}&=\pi_{M,d^k}\!\left(d^k-F^{\mathcal N}(d^k,t_k)\right),\\ \mathcal C^{k-1}&=\varphi\circ\pi_{M,d^k}\!\left(d^k-F^{\mathcal N}(d^k,t_k)\right). \end{aligned}\tag{7}\]

Here, \(z^{t,d_t}\) is a Gaussian vector in the tangent space. Negating the entire noise vector preserves its distribution; the notation here follows the paper’s subtraction of \(F^{\mathcal N}\). The projection \(\pi_M\) ensures realizable distances, and \(\varphi\) selects coordinate representatives.

Together, these operations form metric MDS

\[\begin{aligned} \widehat d&=d+\alpha,\\ \varphi\circ\pi_{M,d}(\widehat d)&\in\underset{\mathcal C\in N}{\operatorname{argmin}}\; \sum_{i<j}\left(\|x_i-x_j\|-\widehat d_{ij}\right)^2. \end{aligned}\tag{8}\]

This objective makes the reconstructed coordinates’ distances as close as possible to the proposal. In implementation, the gauge constraint \(\mathcal C\in N\) can be removed because global rotations and translations do not change the objective. The optimization remains nonlinear, and solving it at every sampling step adds iterations.

Spectral coordinates explain what a “representative” means

For feasible distances, double-centering the squared distances gives a Gram matrix:

\[H=I-\frac1n\mathbf1\mathbf1^\top,\quad B=-\frac12H(d\odot d)H,\quad B=U\Lambda U^\top,\quad\mathcal C_{\mathrm{spec}}=U_3\Lambda_3^{1/2}.\tag{9}\]

This construction requires squared distances. Feasible three-dimensional distances correspond to \(B\succeq0\) and \(\operatorname{rank}(B)\leq3\). Eigenvector signs, repeated eigenvalues, and reflections affect the choice of representative, so \(\varphi\) is used only under a suitable local gauge. Classical MDS with eigenvalue truncation for an infeasible proposal is also different from solving the metric MDS problem in Eq. (8) exactly.

A local linear replacement

04 Approximate expensive projection with small displacements along edges

When an edge should become longer, its endpoints should move in opposite directions along their connecting line. Moving either endpoint also changes adjacent edges. The method scales the displacement according to the number of neighbors to limit these effects, then sums the contributions from all edges.

Step 1: differentiate distance with respect to endpoint coordinates

\[u_{ij}=\frac{x_i-x_j}{d_{ij}},\qquad \frac{\partial d_{ij}}{\partial x_i}=u_{ij},\quad \frac{\partial d_{ij}}{\partial x_j}=-u_{ij},\quad \frac{\partial d_{ij}}{\partial x_\ell}=0\;(\ell\notin\{i,j\}).\tag{10}\]

These derivatives apply only when \(d_{ij}>0\). Each edge produces equal and opposite displacements, so their sum is zero. Rotating the coordinates also rotates the direction vectors.

Step 2: perturb a single edge to obtain a candidate update

Let \(\bar e_{uv}\) have entries equal to 1 only at \((u,v)\) and \((v,u)\). For \(\widehat d=d+\delta\bar e_{uv}\), the paper uses:

\[\begin{aligned} \widehat{\mathcal C}&=\mathcal C+\frac{\delta}{2(n-1)}\frac{\partial d_{uv}}{\partial\mathcal C},\\ \widehat x_u&=x_u+\frac{\delta}{2(n-1)}u_{uv},\qquad \widehat x_v=x_v-\frac{\delta}{2(n-1)}u_{uv}. \end{aligned}\tag{11}\]

With just two points, each endpoint moves by \(\delta/2\), exactly realizing the distance change. With more points, \(1/(n-1)\) reduces the movement to account for constraints from adjacent edges. This is a local approximation, rather than the exact inverse of the distance Jacobian for arbitrary configurations.

Step 3: derive the error bound while keeping its scope clear

Let \(E(\widehat{\mathcal C};\widehat d)\) be the distance residual in Eq. (8). The target edge changes by \(\delta/(n-1)\), leaving a residual of \(-\delta(n-2)/(n-1)\). At most \(2(n-2)\) other edges are affected, each changing by no more than \(|\delta|/[2(n-1)]\). For small perturbations without endpoint crossing:

\[\begin{aligned} 0\leq\min_{\mathcal C'}E(\mathcal C';\widehat d) &\leq E(\widehat{\mathcal C};\widehat d)\\ &\leq\left(\frac{n-2}{n-1}\right)^2\delta^2 +2(n-2)\left(\frac{\delta}{2(n-1)}\right)^2\\ &=\frac{2n^2-7n+6}{2(n-1)^2}\delta^2. \end{aligned}\tag{12}\]

The coefficient approaches 1 as \(n\to\infty\), rather than diverging merely because the number of points grows. The bound controls the candidate objective value under a single-edge perturbation. It does not guarantee coordinate error, global optimality, or error along an entire sampling trajectory; multiple perturbed edges also introduce cross effects.

Step 4: superpose the edge contributions to coordinates

\[\begin{aligned} A\circledast\frac{\partial d}{\partial\mathcal C} &:=\frac12\sum_{i,j}A_{ij}\frac{\partial d_{ij}}{\partial\mathcal C} =\sum_{i<j}A_{ij}\frac{\partial d_{ij}}{\partial\mathcal C},\\ \Delta\mathcal C&\approx\frac1{2(n-1)}\alpha\circledast\frac{\partial d}{\partial\mathcal C},\\ \Delta x_i&\approx\frac1{2(n-1)}\sum_{j\ne i}\alpha_{ij}u_{ij}. \end{aligned}\tag{13}\]

Here, \(A\) contains symmetric edge weights, and \(\circledast\) avoids counting \((i,j)\) and \((j,i)\) twice. For small proposals involving multiple edges, superposing the single-edge operators gives a first-order substitute that turns scalar edge signals into a three-dimensional vector at each point.

\[\mathcal C^{k-1}\approx\mathcal C^k- \frac{F^{\mathcal N}(d^k,t_k)}{2(n-1)}\circledast\frac{\partial d^k}{\partial\mathcal C^k}.\tag{14}\]
“Projection-free” describes the implementation

Only coordinates are updated, so distances recomputed from them are automatically realizable, removing the need for explicit nonlinear distance projection. The desired distance direction is still approximated; the geometric constraints remain.

Why is the chain rule alone insufficient?

Vectorize the distances and write \(J=\partial d/\partial\operatorname{vec}(\mathcal C)\). Local linearization turns distance reconstruction into:

\[\begin{aligned} \operatorname{adj}(\mathcal C+\Delta\mathcal C)&\approx d+J\Delta\mathcal C,\\ \min_{\Delta\mathcal C}\|J\Delta\mathcal C-\alpha\|^2 &\ \Longrightarrow\ J^\top J\Delta\mathcal C=J^\top\alpha,\\ \Delta\mathcal C_{\min\text{-}\mathrm{norm}}&=J^+\alpha. \end{aligned}\]

Here, \(\Delta\mathcal C\) is interpreted as a vector, with rigid-body degrees of freedom fixed. The exact local minimum-norm solution involves the pseudoinverse \(J^+\), whereas backpropagating edge signals gives \(J^\top\alpha\). The paper replaces the more expensive coupled solve with a scaled transpose operator; in general, \(J^+=J^\top/[2(n-1)]\) does not hold.

Lifting the reverse SDE

05 Lift the reverse process from distances back to coordinates

The local conversion describes how a score moves coordinates. The next step uses the known forward coordinate-noising process to replace drift and random terms that are difficult to compute directly in distance space, yielding the paper’s proposed reverse coordinate SDE.

Substituting Eq. (7) into Eq. (14), and writing \(S_t=\nabla_d\log p_t\) at the same time, reveals three contributions:

\[\begin{aligned} \Delta\mathcal C\approx{}&-\frac{\tau f(d_t)}{2(n-1)}\circledast\frac{\partial d_t}{\partial\mathcal C_t}\\ &+\frac{\tau g^2(t)S_t}{2(n-1)}\circledast\frac{\partial d_t}{\partial\mathcal C_t} -\frac{\sqrt\tau g(t)z^{t,d_t}}{2(n-1)}\circledast\frac{\partial d_t}{\partial\mathcal C_t}. \end{aligned}\tag{15}\]

The first term reverses the forward drift; the second denoises along the learned distance score; the third supplies randomness. Using the paper’s local correspondence between the forward coordinate and distance processes, substituting the coordinate drift and noise gives the proposed form:

\[\mathrm d\mathcal C_t\approx \left[\mathbf f(\mathcal C_t,t)-\frac{g^2(t)}{2(n-1)} S_t(d_t)\circledast\frac{\partial d_t}{\partial\mathcal C_t}\right]\mathrm dt +\mathbf G(\mathcal C_t,t)\,\mathrm d\overline W_t.\tag{16}\]

Equation (16) corresponds to the paper’s reverse coordinate SDE. The approximation sign records the approximation already introduced by the local map. Here, \(g(t)\) belongs to the distance description and \(\mathbf G\) to the coordinate description. This expression is not a general time-reversal theorem for arbitrary state-dependent diffusion matrices.

Intuition: distances specify the change; current coordinates supply the direction

The network supplies scalar signals for edge-length changes; \(\partial d_{ij}/\partial\mathcal C\) converts them into pushes and pulls along current edges. The resulting update rotates with the structure without explicitly fixing its orientation.

Radial drift and the ODE approximation

06 Gaussian coordinate noise induces radial drift in distances

A distance is the norm of a vector. Even zero-mean coordinate noise systematically increases that norm, and distance increments sharing an endpoint can be correlated. These two facts explain the reciprocal-distance term in the paper’s ODE and the approximations behind its practical form.

Derive the exact distance change from a coordinate perturbation

Let \(r=x_u-x_v\) and \(\eta\sim\mathcal N(0,2G^2(t)I_3)\). Over a time step \(\tau\), the relative coordinate becomes \(r+\sqrt\tau\eta\). Factoring a difference of squares gives:

\[d_{uv}^{t+\tau}-d_{uv}^t =\frac{2\sqrt\tau\eta^\top r+\tau\|\eta\|^2} {\|r+\sqrt\tau\eta\|+\|r\|}.\tag{17}\]

This is the starting point for analyzing distance transitions. The denominator depends on the same noise, so computing the first moment requires retaining the corresponding correlations rather than treating it as a constant.

The moments and ODE form reported in the paper

The arXiv version uses the following coefficients in its distance-moment approximation, then neglects inter-edge dependence to obtain an edgewise ODE:

\[\begin{aligned} \mathbb E[d_{uv}^t-d_{uv}^{t+\tau}]&\approx-\frac{3\tau G^2(t)}{d_{uv}^t}+o(\tau),\\ \mathbb E[(d_{uv}^{t+\tau}-d_{uv}^t)^2]&=2\tau G^2(t)+o(\tau),\\ \frac{\mathrm dd_t}{\mathrm dt}&\approx G^2(t)\left(\frac3{d_t}-S_t(d_t)\right),\\ \frac{\mathrm d\mathcal C_t}{\mathrm dt}&\approx\frac{G^2(t)}{2(n-1)} \left(\frac3{d_t}-S_t(d_t)\right)\circledast\frac{\partial d_t}{\partial\mathcal C_t}. \end{aligned}\tag{18}\]

Reciprocals and division are evaluated elementwise only for off-diagonal distances. The term \(3/d_t\) represents radial drift, while the score supplies distributional guidance; the same local operator then lifts these signals to coordinates. This reproduces the distance moments and ODE in the paper’s main text, rather than asserting an exact joint probability flow induced by independent coordinate VE noise.

Mathematical detail: coefficients and correlations from Itô’s formula in 3D

Under the stated convention \(\mathrm dx_i=G(t)\mathrm dW_i\), relative noise has covariance \(2G^2I_3\). Writing \(d=\|r\|\) and \(u=r/d\), the gradient and Hessian of the norm are:

\[\nabla_r d=u,\qquad\nabla_r^2d=\frac{I_3-uu^\top}{d},\qquad \frac12\operatorname{tr}\!\left(2G^2I_3\nabla_r^2d\right)=\frac{2G^2}{d}.\]

Combining this second-order drift with the first-order random term, Itô’s formula gives:

\[\begin{aligned} \mathrm dd_{ij}&=\frac{2G^2(t)}{d_{ij}}\,\mathrm dt +G(t)u_{ij}^\top(\mathrm dW_i-\mathrm dW_j),\\ \mathbb E[\Delta d_{ij}]&=\frac{2\tau G^2(t)}{d_{ij}}+o(\tau),\qquad \mathbb E[(\Delta d_{ij})^2]=2\tau G^2(t)+o(\tau). \end{aligned}\tag{19}\]

Thus, under this noise convention, the exact single-edge radial drift coefficient is 2, differing from the paper’s coefficient 3 in Eq. (18). Retaining the denominator correlations in the second-order expansion gives this correction. For the length \(\rho\) of a single edge, its probability flow is:

\[\dot\rho=\frac{2G^2(t)}\rho-G^2(t)\partial_\rho\log p_t^{ij}(\rho).\]

Here, \(p_t^{ij}\) is the marginal density of this edge length. Individual edge probability flows still cannot be assembled directly into independent updates of the joint distances.

Let \(J\) be the distance Jacobian from Eq. (10). The covariance rate of the joint distance increments is:

\[\begin{aligned} A&=G^2JJ^\top,\\ A_{(ij),(k\ell)}&=G^2(\delta_{ik}-\delta_{i\ell}-\delta_{jk}+\delta_{j\ell}) u_{ij}^\top u_{k\ell}. \end{aligned}\tag{20}\]

An edge has variance rate \(2G^2\), and edges sharing a point generally have nonzero correlations. Complete distances also form a redundant representation, so an ordinary full-dimensional density over the Euclidean space of all edges cannot be assumed. Choosing independent local variables \(y\) in a regular region, and using their coordinate density, gives the general Fokker–Planck and probability-flow relation:

\[\begin{aligned} \partial_t p&=-\sum_a\partial_a(b_ap)+\frac12\sum_{a,b}\partial_a\partial_b(A_{ab}p),\\ v_a&=b_a-\frac1{2p}\sum_b\partial_b(A_{ab}p) =b_a-\frac12(\nabla\!\cdot A)_a-\frac12(A\nabla\log p)_a. \end{aligned}\tag{21}\]

In Eq. (21), \(b,A\) are the drift and covariance of the chosen local variables. The matrix for redundant distances cannot simply be reused as their coefficients. Isotropic coordinate noise therefore does not automatically imply independent, isotropic distance noise.

The practical ODE also approximates away the explicit reciprocal-distance term

To avoid dividing by very small distances, the paper uses a Gaussian distance-score approximation and omits the explicit radial drift in Eq. (18), arriving at:

\[\frac{\mathrm d\mathcal C_t}{\mathrm dt}\approx -\frac{G^2(t)}{2(n-1)}S_t(d_t)\circledast\frac{\partial d_t}{\partial\mathcal C_t}.\tag{22}\]

This gives a deterministic sampling direction that is convenient to discretize and accelerate. It relies on approximations to the distance distribution, inter-edge dependence, and geometric lifting, so it does not establish exact equality of all marginals with the original coordinate diffusion. The corrector introduced below empirically compensates for these accumulated biases.

Learning edge signals on sparse graphs

07 Learn distance denoising signals with a loss on coordinate vectors

The network does not need to predict an absolute orientation. It extracts invariant features from distances and structural conditions, predicts denoising signals on edges, and combines them with geometric directions to form coordinate vectors. Training compares the resulting vectors directly with their targets.

From a radial distribution assumption to computable supervision

The paper combines a Gaussian term with a Maxwell–Boltzmann-type radial term to approximate the conditional distance score:

\[S_t^{\mathrm{cond}}(d_t,d_0) :=\nabla_d\log p_t(d_t\mid d_0) \approx\frac{a_t}{d_t}-\frac{d_t-d_0}{2\sigma_t^2}.\tag{23}\]

The hyperparameter \(a_t\) controls the radial contribution. Relative-coordinate noise for two points has variance \(2\sigma_t^2\), accounting for the factor 2 in the denominator. The true conditional length of a single edge follows a noncentral chi distribution, and different edges are correlated, so Eq. (23) is a modeling assumption.

To remove the unstable \(1/d_t\) term, the practical supervision becomes:

\[\widetilde S_t^{\mathrm{cond}}=-\frac{d_t-d_0}{2\sigma_t^2}.\tag{24}\]

Intuitively, measure how far each noisy distance is from its clean value, normalize by the noise scale, and pull it back. Equation (24) approximates distance changes as Gaussian; it is not the exact score of Gaussian coordinate noise.

Message passing and a coordinate-space loss

\[h_u^{(m+1)}=\psi\!\left(h_u^{(m)}, \sum_{v\in\mathcal N(u)}h_v^{(m)}\cdot\phi(e_{uv},d_{uv})\right).\tag{25}\]

Messages combine edge features \(e_{uv}\), distances, and node representations; a molecular graph or pose category supplies task conditions. The experimental backbone does not explicitly embed diffusion time. The symbol \(t\) below labels the current noise level without requiring time to be a network input. Backpropagating predicted edge signals through Eq. (10) gives an \(n\times3\) vector field \(s_\theta(d_t,t)\). The paper’s loss is:

\[\begin{aligned} \ell(\theta;d_t,d_0)&=\left\|s_\theta(d_t,t)- \widetilde S_t^{\mathrm{cond}}\circledast\frac{\partial d_t}{\partial\mathcal C_t}\right\|_F^2,\\ \mathcal L(\theta)&=\frac1{N_\sigma}\sum_{t=1}^{N_\sigma}\frac{\sigma_t}{2} \mathbb E_{d_0,d_t}[\ell(\theta;d_t,d_0)]. \end{aligned}\tag{26}\]

Here, \(s_\theta\) denotes the coordinate vectors after lifting, rather than a raw \(n\times n\) distance score. With exact conditional scores and an exact map, the conditional expectation of a denoising target has the standard connection to the marginal score. Using Eq. (24) instead trains the network on the corresponding approximate target. Scaling can be placed in the sampler or target, but the implementation must remain consistent.

Sparse graphs: replace complete-graph normalization with local degree normalization

Practical message passing uses only a subset of edges. For symmetric edge weights \(S_{ij}\), generalize the complete-graph lifting factor \(1/[2(n-1)]\) as:

\[v_i^{E}(S)=\frac1{2\deg(i)}\sum_{j\in\mathcal N(i)} S_{ij}\frac{x_i-x_j}{d_{ij}}.\tag{27}\]

Equation (27) explicitly aggregates at each node. For a complete graph, \(\deg(i)=n-1\), recovering the nodewise normalization in Eq. (13). The paper’s sparse expression combines full-coordinate derivatives with a sum over both edge directions, leaving differences in counting conventions; the nodewise form here makes the implementation explicit. On graphs with unequal degrees, this remains an empirical approximation, and network outputs, sampling coefficients, and the corrector must share a consistent normalization. If centering is required at each step, coordinates can be recentered after the update without changing distances.

Fewer edges reduce computation but may remove constraints needed to determine a structure. A minimum degree of at least 4 alone does not guarantee uniqueness for an arbitrary three-dimensional graph. Local recoverability involves rigidity; global uniqueness additionally involves global rigidity and reflection ambiguity.

Compensating accumulated approximation error

08 Use a corrector to adjust the effective denoising step

Local lifting, neglected edge correlations, and approximate distance scores all introduce bias. The corrector starts from the idea that a nominal denoising step may fall short of its target noise level. Amplifying the drift can bring the actual progress closer to the intended progress.

Write prediction error explicitly into an update

Let \(v_\theta\) denote the predicted vector including the \(1/[2(n-1)]\) normalization. Under a consistent complete-graph convention, \(v_\theta=s_\theta/[2(n-1)]\); sparse graphs use their corresponding degree normalization. To analyze the effect, the paper models prediction error as having Gaussian-like behavior:

\[v_\theta(d_t,t)= \frac1{2(n-1)}S_t(d_t)\circledast\frac{\partial d_t}{\partial\mathcal C_t} +\epsilon(d_t,t).\tag{28}\]

Moving from a higher-noise time \(s\) to \(t<s\), define \(\Delta\sigma^2=\sigma_s^2-\sigma_t^2>0\). Freeze the current vector field and integrate Eq. (22):

\[\begin{aligned} \mathcal C_t&\approx\mathcal C_s+ \Delta\sigma^2\left[v_\theta(d_s,s)-\epsilon(d_s,s)\right],\\ \mathcal C_t^{\mathrm{model}}&=\mathcal C_s+\Delta\sigma^2v_\theta(d_s,s). \end{aligned}\tag{29}\]

The ideal step and the model step differ by \(\Delta\sigma^2\epsilon\). Under the paper’s error model, this can be interpreted as residual extra noise: samples intended for time \(t\) still resemble a distribution at a higher noise level. This is an explanatory model, rather than a theorem that arbitrary prediction errors are equivalent to a time shift.

Implement the compensation as a multiplicative coefficient

\[\mathcal C_t\approx\mathcal C_s+ \kappa_\theta(d_s,s,t)\left(\sigma_s^2-\sigma_t^2\right)v_\theta(d_s,s), \qquad\kappa_\theta>1.\tag{30}\]

The experiments further approximate \(\kappa_\theta\) by a constant for each dataset, selected through search. This corrector is a drift amplification coefficient, rather than an additional Langevin chain. The denoising drift in the SDE can receive corresponding calibration, but the random term cannot simply be amplified by the same factor.

Why it helps, and why it needs tuning

A larger coefficient can compensate for insufficient denoising while also amplifying directional errors and affecting stability. The corrector supports more effective sampling with fewer steps, but does not guarantee recovery of the exact probability flow or removal of every geometric approximation.

A practical ODE step therefore follows a clear sequence: compute distances from current coordinates → predict edge signals → aggregate along edge directions and normalize → update coordinates using the noise-variance difference and corrector → recompute distances. Each step maintains three-dimensional coordinates without an additional metric MDS solve.

Evidence across molecules and human poses

09 Experiments test whether the same model can sample more effectively

Molecular conformation and human pose experiments compare sampling rules, measuring coverage, matching error, and structural quality as the number of steps decreases. Fewer steps mean fewer updates; actual runtime gains also depend on the cost per step.

Baseline: Langevin dynamics with a tuned step size

\[\mathcal C_{k-1}=\mathcal C_k+ \alpha_k\widehat s_k(\mathcal C_k)+\sqrt{2\alpha_k}z_k, \qquad\alpha_k=\delta\sigma_k^2,\quad z_k\sim\mathcal N(0,I).\tag{31}\]

The LD step-size parameter \(\delta\) and the new samplers’ hyperparameters are all searched. Molecular experiments keep the trained model fixed while varying the sampling rule and number of steps. The 5000 training noise levels reported in the paper should not be interpreted as only 5000 optimization updates.

How does molecular shape emerge from random coordinates?

Molecular conformations: examine recall and precision together

Let \(S_r\) be the reference conformation set, \(S_g\) the generated set, \(\rho\) the RMSD after structural alignment, and \(\delta_0\) the coverage threshold. The two metric directions are:

\[\begin{aligned} \text{COV-R}&=\frac{\bigl|\{X\in S_r:\min_{Y\in S_g}\rho(X,Y)<\delta_0\}\bigr|}{|S_r|},\\ \text{MAT-R}&=\frac1{|S_r|}\sum_{X\in S_r}\min_{Y\in S_g}\rho(X,Y),\\ \text{COV-P}&=\frac{\bigl|\{Y\in S_g:\min_{X\in S_r}\rho(X,Y)<\delta_0\}\bigr|}{|S_g|},\\ \text{MAT-P}&=\frac1{|S_g|}\sum_{Y\in S_g}\min_{X\in S_r}\rho(X,Y). \end{aligned}\tag{32}\]

Higher COV and lower MAT are better. R measures how well reference structures are covered; P measures how closely generated structures match the reference set. The coverage thresholds are 0.5 Å for QM9 and 1.25 Å for Drugs.

Human poses: how far are aligned samples from the reference set?

The Human3.6M experiments represent a pose with 17 keypoints; the main comparison generates 500 poses from the eating category. AD compares each generated sample with its closest aligned pose in the reference set:

\[\operatorname{AD}=\frac1{|S_g|}\sum_{X\in S_g}\min_{X_r\in S_r} \|\operatorname{Align}(X,X_r)-X_r\|_F^2.\tag{33}\]

Align minimizes structural differences over rotations and translations; lower AD is better. This is a one-way nearest-neighbor match from generated samples to references, so it alone cannot establish diversity or complete coverage of the pose distribution.

What the geometric view contributes

10 Make the geometric conversion explicit to understand the sampling direction

The central connection is that coordinate noise produces feasible distances, the distance score describes changes in internal structure, and a local operator along edges turns those signals into coordinate updates. Geometry previously hidden inside projection becomes a sequence of operations that can be analyzed, learned, and calibrated.

\[\mathcal C_t\xrightarrow{\operatorname{adj}}d_t \xrightarrow{\text{distance score}}S_t \xrightarrow{\;\frac1{2(n-1)}\circledast\frac{\partial d_t}{\partial\mathcal C_t}\;} v_t\xrightarrow{\text{SDE / ODE step}}\mathcal C_{t-\tau}.\tag{34}\]
  • A local operator replaces explicit projection. It reduces the geometric solve required at each step. The single-edge residual bound describes a controlled approximation, rather than a global trajectory guarantee.
  • Radial drift and edge correlations are central to distance diffusion. Independent Gaussian coordinate noise does not remain an independent Gaussian process after mapping vectors to their lengths. Constructing a probability flow must account for these geometric effects.
  • The training target and sampler must agree. The Gaussian distance score, sparse normalization, and corrector jointly determine the practical vector field. The corrector provides empirical compensation, rather than an exact distributional analysis.
  • Distance representations have limits. Reflections, degenerate configurations, and sparse-graph rigidity affect geometric recoverability. Generating chiral structures requires additional information.
  • Specific metrics support specific claims. The experiments show more effective sampling with fewer steps using the same model, while also revealing trade-offs among recall, precision, and sampling budget.
