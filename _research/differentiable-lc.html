The inverse problem

01 Inferring column parameters from an outlet curve

A gradient program controls how the solvent composition changes over time. The instrument records a curve at the column outlet, and the study uses it to estimate nine physical parameters that govern how the sample moves and adsorbs inside the column.

The forward problem predicts the curve from a set of parameters. The inverse problem starts with the measured curve and searches for the parameters. Every candidate needs a new prediction, so the cost of the forward solve adds up quickly.

Learning the forward model

02 Two neural surrogates for faster forward solves

The study first solves the general rate model (GRM) in COMSOL for a range of parameters and gradient programs. Both networks train on those simulated concentration fields so they can predict outlet curves quickly during inversion.

PINN · Query concentration at a point in space and time

The physics-informed neural network (PINN) takes physical parameters, experimental conditions, and space-time coordinates. It predicts local concentrations in the bulk liquid, within particle pores, and in the adsorbed phase. During training, it fits the simulation data and uses automatic differentiation to compute spatial and temporal derivatives. These derivatives help evaluate how closely the predictions satisfy the governing equations, initial conditions, and boundary conditions.

FNO · Advance the entire concentration field one step at a time

The Fourier neural operator (FNO) takes the current concentration field on a grid, the physical parameters, and the experimental conditions. Its Fourier layers mix information along the column axis to predict the field at the next time step. During training, the FNO feeds its predictions back into the network and compares the resulting trajectory with COMSOL across multiple steps. The training error covers the bulk liquid, particle pores, and adsorbed phase.

Inverse solvers

03 Searching for parameters that fit the measured curves

Both workflows fix the network weights and the known experimental programs, then search for one set of physical parameters that explains several measured curves. They differ in how they choose the next candidates.

PINN + L-BFGS · Update one parameter set using gradients

The solver squares the difference at each sampled time, averages over the curve, and then averages across the experimental programs. Automatic differentiation gives the gradient of that error with respect to the nine physical parameters. L-BFGS uses recent changes in the parameters and gradients to approximate the local curvature and choose an update.

Across 300 simulated parameter sets, PINN with L-BFGS took about 6 minutes per inversion on average, versus about 7.5 hours for the derivative-free Bayesian optimization baseline. With the same objective, the mean relative parameter errors were 7.37% and 26.62%, respectively.

FNO + genetic algorithm · Screen candidates in batches

Because local gradient optimization with the FNO was sensitive to initialization, the study used a genetic algorithm to search across many candidates at once. In each generation, the FNO predicts curves for all candidates under three gradient programs in batches. The search scores curve shape, peak time, and peak height, then selects, recombines, and mutates promising candidates to form the next generation.

The representative experiments started with 1,024 candidates and ran for 15 generations. On an RTX A6000 with batches of 256 trajectories, the network completed 138.5 360-step forward rollouts per second. This rate measures FNO rollouts alone. The genetic search and COMSOL re-simulations have separate costs.

Wet-lab validation

04 Can the fitted parameters explain new experiments?

Both workflows estimate parameters from measured gradient chromatograms and use the GRM to predict curves under fixed solvent compositions. Independent isocratic experiments test how those parameters perform under new conditions.

PINN · Reuse the fitted parameters directly

The parameters fitted to gradient data reproduce the isocratic curves for most representative samples. The weakly retained sample G03-33 elutes early, with its outlet peaks clustered in a short time window even across different gradient programs. The simulations roughly match the peak times under those programs, yet predict an isocratic peak well before the green measured peak near 300 seconds.

FNO · Validate after adjusting the desorption constant

For the isocratic experiments, the study doubled the fitted desorption constant, which governs how quickly the sample leaves the particle surface, and retained the other fitted values. The retention-time RMSE across nine gradient curves for three representative compounds was 6.3 seconds. With the adjustment, the RMSE across 13 independent isocratic curves with measurable peaks was 34.7 seconds.
