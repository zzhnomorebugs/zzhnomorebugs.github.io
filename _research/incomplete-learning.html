Learning from never-complete observations

01 Can a model learn to complete a field if the training set never contains a complete answer?

Clouds obscure the sea surface, satellites sample along tracks, and sensors cover only a few locations. Missingness is present during training as well as inference: every training field is incomplete, and its originally missing ground truth cannot be recovered.

Figure 1 · Examples of original observations, with one sample from each of three ocean datasets. Colored locations have measurements, white locations have no source data, and gray marks a fixed unobserved domain. These gaps already exist in the training data; they are not answers additionally hidden to demonstrate completion. Colors show the field values of each product, and the three samples do not share physical units.

  1. Input: an incomplete training setEach sample contains only observed values and their locations.
  2. Learning: relationships between fragmentsBuild supervision from co-occurring observations to learn spatial and spatiotemporal structure.
  3. Output: a reconstructed field for a new samplePreserve the supplied observations and estimate missing values within the valid domain.

Conventional completion training starts with complete data, artificially hides some values, and computes error against the complete answer. No such complete training target is available here. Repeated training on the same sample can change only which existing observations are supplied to the model; values that were never measured cannot become supervision.

\[ \boldsymbol{x}_{\mathrm{obs}}=\boldsymbol{M}\odot\boldsymbol{x}_0, \qquad \boldsymbol{M}\le\boldsymbol{V},\qquad \boldsymbol{M}\ne\boldsymbol{V}. \]

\(\boldsymbol{x}_0\) is the conceptual complete field, \(\boldsymbol{M}\) marks actual observations, and \(\boldsymbol{V}\) defines the valid physical domain; locations outside that domain are not reconstruction targets. Zero filling is only a storage convention, so the model needs both values and masks to distinguish a measured zero from an unmeasured value.

The central question across this research direction

How can incomplete observations provide effective supervision, avoid learning dead zones caused by missingness patterns, and support better reconstruction rules when evidence is limited? This requires understanding the supervision target, designing observation partitions, and organizing how models learn local and global structure.

The context–query framework: training and sampling

02 A context–query framework connecting training, reconstruction, and sampling

Error cannot be computed at truly missing locations, but existing measurements can be temporarily hidden. Incomplete Data, Complete Dynamics (IDCD) uses this idea to train a field predictor, then combines predictions from multiple contexts at inference for point reconstruction or multistep generation.

Consider an intuitive example: blue observations enter the model, while orange observations are temporarily hidden as query answers. The top row partitions pixels, and the bottom row partitions whole blocks. The partition determines whether other samples can provide supervision for the currently missing regions.

  1. Training: supervision from existing observationsContext serves as input; real measurements at query locations provide the answers.
  2. Reconstruction: ensemble subcontext predictionsRepeatedly sample contexts near zero noise, average their predictions, and restore the supplied observations.
  3. Generation: stepwise reverse updatesUse ensemble predictions at each reverse diffusion step to generate possible completions.

Training: turn some existing observations into questions and others into answers

First choose \(\boldsymbol M_{\mathrm{ctx}},\boldsymbol M_{\mathrm{qry}}\le\boldsymbol M\). The general IDCD analysis allows them to overlap and does not assume they must be exhaustive or disjoint. The observation-aligned approach introduced later uses an explicit disjoint partition, \(\boldsymbol M_{\mathrm{qry}}=\boldsymbol M\odot(1-\boldsymbol M_{\mathrm{ctx}})\). All supervised values come from the original observable support.

\[ \begin{aligned} \boldsymbol{x}_{\mathrm{obs},t} &=\boldsymbol M\odot(\alpha_t\boldsymbol{x}_{\mathrm{obs}}+\sigma_t\boldsymbol\epsilon), &&\boldsymbol\epsilon\sim\mathcal N(0,I),\\ O_t&=(\boldsymbol{x}_{\mathrm{ctx},t},\boldsymbol M_{\mathrm{ctx}}), &&\boldsymbol{x}_{\mathrm{ctx},t}=\boldsymbol M_{\mathrm{ctx}}\odot\boldsymbol{x}_{\mathrm{obs},t},\\ \mathcal L_{\mathrm{qry}}(\theta) &=\mathbb E\!\left[\left\|\boldsymbol M_{\mathrm{qry}}\odot \left(f_\theta(t;O_t)-\boldsymbol{x}_{\mathrm{obs}}\right)\right\|_2^2\right]. \end{aligned} \]

The network predicts the clean field and receives only time, noisy context, and the context mask. The query mask remains on the loss side. This arrangement lets all valid queries associated with a context jointly constrain its prediction, rather than allowing the model to treat known scoring locations specially.

What exactly does squared loss learn?

We first need to distinguish general query selection from the special case of mask independence. Real observations may depend on the field itself. Only under the corresponding conditions can the conditional mean given a query event simplify to the commonly stated conditional mean given the context.

Theorem 1 · The optimum of coordinatewise weighted mean squared error

Let random vectors \(X,Y,Z\in\mathbb R^d\) have finite relevant second moments, and let \(g:\operatorname{Space}(Y)\to\mathbb R^d\) be a deterministic measurable function. Consider the objective

\[ L(g)=\mathbb E\left[\|Z\odot g(Y)-Z\odot X\|^2\right]. \]

If every coordinate of \(\mathbb E[Z\odot Z\mid Y]\) is strictly positive almost everywhere, the unique optimal function, up to almost-everywhere equality, is

\[ g^*(Y)=\frac{\mathbb E[Z\odot Z\odot X\mid Y]}{\mathbb E[Z\odot Z\mid Y]},\qquad g_i^*(Y)=\frac{\mathbb E[Z_i^2X_i\mid Y]}{\mathbb E[Z_i^2\mid Y]}. \]

Vector division is coordinatewise. If a coordinate has a zero denominator, its output does not affect the objective and therefore cannot be determined by this loss.

Theorem 2 · Query loss identifies the query-conditional mean

Let \(O_t=(\boldsymbol{x}_{\mathrm{ctx},t},\boldsymbol M_{\mathrm{ctx}})\), with the predictor receiving only \((t,O_t)\). For coordinate \(i\), assume finite relevant second moments and positive query exposure in the current state, \(\Pr(\boldsymbol M_{\mathrm{qry},i}=1\mid t,O_t)>0\). For the masked squared loss on observed queries, the unrestricted predictor has optimum

\[\mu_{O,i}(t,O_t)=\mathbb E_{\mathrm{train}}\!\left[\boldsymbol{x}_{0,i}\mid t,O_t,\boldsymbol M_{\mathrm{qry},i}=1\right].\]

Here \(\boldsymbol{x}_{0,i}\) can be a scalar or a channel vector. No additional independence condition that removes the query-selection event is assumed for the actual acquisition and partition mechanisms, so \(\boldsymbol M_{\mathrm{qry},i}=1\) must be retained. If conditional query exposure is zero, the loss does not constrain the prediction of that coordinate in that state.

Theorem 3 · Optimal prediction and learning frequency when query information is hidden

Let the complete data be \(X_0\) and the original observations be \(X_{\mathrm{obs}}=M\odot X_0\), where \(M\) is independent of the data. Choose \(C=M_{\mathrm{ctx}}\subseteq M\) and \(Q=M_{\mathrm{qry}}\subseteq M\), with mask sampling depending only on observation structure. The model receives only \(t\) and \(\mathrm{ctx}=(C\odot X_{\mathrm{obs},t},C)\), without \(Q\) or the original \(M\). Let

\[ X_{\mathrm{obs},t}=M\odot(\alpha_tX_{\mathrm{obs}}+\sigma_t\epsilon),\quad \epsilon\sim\mathcal N(0,I),\qquad \mathcal L=\|Q\odot(x_\theta(t,\mathrm{ctx})-X_{\mathrm{obs}})\|^2. \]

Assume \(x_\theta^*\) attains the optimum of the population expected loss, the relevant second moments are finite, and \(X_0\) and \(Q\) are conditionally independent given \(\mathrm{ctx}\). Fix \(C\), write \(p_i=\mathbb P(Q_i=1\mid C)\), and require \(\mathbb P(Q_i=1\mid\mathrm{ctx})=p_i\). Then:

(i) Optimal output.

\[ (x_\theta^*(t,\mathrm{ctx}))_i= \begin{cases} \mathbb E[(X_0)_i\mid\mathrm{ctx}],&p_i>0,\\ \text{arbitrary},&p_i=0. \end{cases} \]

In particular, for a given \(C\), if the union of the supports of all possible query masks covers every coordinate, namely \(\bigcup_{Q:\,\mathbb P(Q\mid C)>0}\operatorname{supp}(Q)=\{1,\ldots,d\}\), then

\[ x_\theta^*(t,\mathrm{ctx})=\mathbb E[X_0\mid\mathrm{ctx}]. \]

(ii) Gradient magnitude. Under the sampling distribution for a fixed context mask, when \(p_i>0\),

\[ \frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial(x_\theta)_i}=2Q_i((x_\theta)_i-(X_{\mathrm{obs}})_i), \] \[ \mathbb E\left[\left(\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial(x_\theta)_i}\right)^2\right] =4p_i\mathbb E\left[((x_\theta)_i-(X_{\mathrm{obs}})_i)^2\mid Q_i=1\right]. \]

Holding the conditional error factor fixed, the expected squared gradient varies linearly with \(p_i\). If \(p_i=0\), the loss gradient for that coordinate is always zero, so no conditional expectation on a zero-probability event needs to be defined.

(iii) Update opportunities. The probability that coordinate \(i\) enters the loss and can contribute to a parameter update is

\[ \mathbb P(\text{coordinate }i\text{ contributes to the parameter update}\mid C) =\mathbb P(Q_i=1\mid C)=p_i. \]

The coordinate's parameter-gradient contribution is \(2Q_i((x_\theta)_i-(X_{\mathrm{obs}})_i)\,\partial(x_\theta)_i/\partial\theta\). If the residual and corresponding Jacobian are nonzero when selected, the frequency of actual nonzero updates also equals \(p_i\). Once the residual is zero, entering the loss does not imply a nonzero update.

Thus, an unobserved location \(i\) in one sample does not mean that every sample with a similar context is unable to supervise \(i\). What matters is whether this location can still be queried under that context in the training distribution. Without that opportunity, the output in that state is unconstrained by the loss.

Sampling: form a reconstruction from predictions under multiple contexts

During training, the model learns to predict from subcontexts, whereas inference provides all supplied observations. IDCD first adds noise near zero once to these observations, then repeatedly samples contexts from the same noisy observations, predicts separately, and averages. This operation primarily reduces variance caused by context sampling.

\[ \widehat{\boldsymbol\mu}_K =\frac1K\sum_{k=1}^K f_\theta \left(\delta;\boldsymbol M_{\mathrm{ctx}}^{(k)}\odot \boldsymbol{x}_{\mathrm{obs},\delta},\boldsymbol M_{\mathrm{ctx}}^{(k)}\right), \qquad 0<\delta\ll1. \]

Here, single-step means one denoising prediction near zero noise; \(K\) contexts still require \(K\) model evaluations. The average fills missing locations within the valid domain, while the supplied real observations are written back to the output. For reconstruction tasks with concentrated posteriors, the conditional mean can serve as a representative answer. Representing multiple possible completions requires further consideration of multistep generation.

Theorem 4 · Ensembling reduces variance while retaining information gaps and systematic bias

Assumption: an uncorrelated decomposition of ensemble error.

Write the model output as

\[ x_\theta(t,\mathrm{ctx})=\mathbb E[X_0\mid\mathrm{ctx}]+b(\mathrm{ctx})+\epsilon_{\mathrm{bias}}(\mathrm{ctx}), \qquad\mathbb E[\epsilon_{\mathrm{bias}}]=0. \]

Here \(b\) is a deterministic bias that varies with context, and \(\epsilon_{\mathrm{bias}}\) is zero-mean random error. All terms have finite second moments. Assume that the following three centered components are pairwise uncorrelated:

\[ \mathbb E[X_0\mid\mathrm{ctx}]-\mathbb E[\mathbb E[X_0\mid\mathrm{ctx}]],\qquad b(\mathrm{ctx})-\mathbb E[b(\mathrm{ctx})],\qquad \epsilon_{\mathrm{bias}}(\mathrm{ctx}). \]

Random errors \(\epsilon_{\mathrm{bias}}(\mathrm{ctx}^{(i)})\) and \(\epsilon_{\mathrm{bias}}(\mathrm{ctx}^{(j)})\) from different ensemble members are independent for \(i\ne j\). For ensemble analysis with a fixed set of all available observations, centering, uncorrelatedness, and zero means below are all understood under the conditional distribution given those observations.

Let \(\mathrm{obs}=(M\odot X_{\mathrm{obs},t},M)\) denote all available observations and \(\mathrm{ctx}=(C\odot X_{\mathrm{obs},t},C)\) a subset, with sampling of \(C\subseteq M\) having no additional dependence on data values. Let

\[ \mu=\mathbb E[X_0\mid\mathrm{obs}],\qquad \mu_c=\mathbb E[X_0\mid\mathrm{ctx}]. \]

If the relevant second moments are finite and the tower relation for nested observations, \(\mathbb E[\mu\mid\mathrm{ctx}]=\mu_c\), holds, then the information lost by the context corresponds to the following mean squared difference:

\[ \mathbb E[\|\mu_c-\mu\|^2] =\mathbb E[\operatorname{Var}(X_0\mid\mathrm{ctx})] -\mathbb E[\operatorname{Var}(X_0\mid\mathrm{obs})]. \]

For vectors, variance here denotes the trace of the covariance matrix, or mean squared Euclidean deviation. Now fix \(\mathrm{obs}\), draw \(K\) context masks independently and identically, and assume the uncorrelated error decomposition above. Let \(\mathbb E_c\) and \(\operatorname{Var}_c\) refer to the distribution of contexts and random model error conditional on these observations, and define

\[ \widehat\mu_K=\frac1K\sum_{k=1}^Kx_\theta(t,\mathrm{ctx}^{(k)}). \]

The mean squared error of the ensemble estimate decomposes exactly as

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb E_c[\|\widehat\mu_K-\mu\|^2] ={}&\left\|\underbrace{\mathbb E_c[\mu_c]-\mu}_{\text{information gap}} +\underbrace{\mathbb E_c[b(\mathrm{ctx})]}_{\text{mean model bias}}\right\|^2\\ &+\frac1K\left(\underbrace{\operatorname{Var}_c(\mu_c)}_{\text{context-induced variance}} +\underbrace{\operatorname{Var}_c(b(\mathrm{ctx}))+\operatorname{Var}_c(\epsilon_{\mathrm{bias}})}_{\text{model variance}}\right). \end{aligned} \]

Therefore,

\[ \lim_{K\to\infty}\mathbb E_c[\|\widehat\mu_K-\mu\|^2] =\left\|\mathbb E_c[\mu_c]-\mu+\mathbb E_c[b(\mathrm{ctx})]\right\|^2. \]

The data variance, variance of context-dependent bias, and random-error variance all shrink as \(1/K\); the mean systematic bias does not disappear with ensembling. The limit is zero only if \(\mathbb E_c[\mu_c]+\mathbb E_c[b]=\mu\).

Ensembling reduces variance without automatically closing the information gap

As \(K\) grows, the variance terms caused by random contexts decrease; an error floor from insufficient context and systematic bias may remain. Better contexts and better estimator structure are needed, beyond increasing the number of predictions averaged.

From single-step reconstruction to multistep generation

The multistep version starts from Gaussian noise. It first fixes the observation-conditioned ensemble prediction near zero noise described above. At each reverse diffusion step, a random mask is then sampled from the current complete generated state to obtain a dynamic denoising prediction, which is combined with the fixed observation-conditioned prediction through a weighted blend. During updates, observed locations use the noise term corresponding to the supplied measurements, while missing locations use the noise term corresponding to the blended prediction. The original observations are still written back at the end. The blending weight is a heuristic implementation choice; the mean and squared-error results above do not themselves guarantee recovery of the complete sampling distribution.

Observation geometry determines supervision

03 Seeing fewer points is not enough: context must not reveal where answers are never available

The partition determines the supervision the model actually receives. Random pixel dropout is natural for pixel-sampled data, but for data with whole missing blocks, it may reveal the original missingness geometry too clearly.

Principle · Every unknown coordinate needs a chance to become a query

Effective context–query sampling should satisfy two conditions. First, every coordinate \(i\) outside the context must have a positive conditional query probability:

\[ C_i=0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\mathbb P(Q_i=1\mid C)>0. \]

Second, to balance the learning frequency across coordinates, these probabilities should be as similar as possible:

\[ \mathbb P(Q_i=1\mid C)\approx\mathbb P(Q_j=1\mid C), \qquad\forall i,j:\ C_i=C_j=0. \]

By the law of total probability,

\[ \mathbb P(Q_i=1\mid C) =\sum_M\mathbb P(Q_i=1\mid C,M)\mathbb P(M\mid C). \]

Thus, for every coordinate to be predicted, the current \(C\) must be compatible with an original mask \(M\) of positive probability in which that coordinate is actually observed and has a chance to be selected as a query. Approximate uniformity is a design goal for balanced learning; positive query probability is a key condition for identifying the corresponding conditional mean in Chapter Two.

From handcrafted rules to intersections of a mask distribution

When the sensing mechanism is known, pixels can be sampled pixelwise and blocks as whole blocks. Real satellite occlusions, however, involve cloud clusters, narrow tracks, and complex boundaries that are difficult to capture with a single rule. A more general approach is to learn the observation-mask distribution and generate context by intersecting two masks. Observation-Aligned Mask Priors (OAMP) makes this step learnable.

Theorem 5 · Conditional query positivity from independent mask intersections

Assumption: complete intersection coverage.

Let the support of the real binary observation-mask distribution be \(S=\{\boldsymbol{x}\in\{0,1\}^d:P(\boldsymbol{M}=\boldsymbol{x})>0\}\). Call \(\boldsymbol{m}\) a valid context mask if and only if there exist \(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}\in S\) such that \(\boldsymbol{x}\odot\boldsymbol{y}=\boldsymbol{m}\). For every such \(\boldsymbol{m}\) and every coordinate with \(m_i=0\), assume that there exist \(\boldsymbol{M}_a,\boldsymbol{M}_b\in S\) satisfying both

\[ \boldsymbol{M}_a\odot\boldsymbol{M}_b=\boldsymbol{m}, \qquad (\boldsymbol{M}_a)_i=1. \]

The first condition requires two masks in the support to generate this context; the second requires the coordinate to appear in at least one of those observations. Permanently unobserved coordinates do not satisfy this assumption.

Let \(\boldsymbol{M}_1,\boldsymbol{M}_2\in\{0,1\}^d\) be sampled independently from the same distribution \(P(\boldsymbol{M})\), and define

\[ \boldsymbol{M}_{\mathrm{ctx}}=\boldsymbol{M}_1\odot\boldsymbol{M}_2, \qquad \boldsymbol{M}_{\mathrm{qry}}=\boldsymbol{M}_1\odot(\boldsymbol{1}-\boldsymbol{M}_{\mathrm{ctx}}). \]

If complete intersection coverage holds, then for every valid context \(\boldsymbol{m}\) and every coordinate with \(m_i=0\),

\[ P\big((\boldsymbol{M}_{\mathrm{qry}})_i=1\mid\boldsymbol{M}_{\mathrm{ctx}}=\boldsymbol{m}\big)>0. \]

Proof sketch: validity ensures a positive denominator, \(P(\boldsymbol{M}_{\mathrm{ctx}}=\boldsymbol{m})>0\). The mask pair in the assumption occurs with probability \(P(\boldsymbol{M}_a)P(\boldsymbol{M}_b)>0\) and, when \(m_i=0\), makes \((M_{\mathrm{qry}})_i=(M_1)_i=1\), so the numerator is also strictly positive. This is a conclusion under support and coverage conditions, rather than an unconditional guarantee for arbitrary mask distributions.

This result shows that sufficiently rich mask support must be retained, beyond a particular average missing rate. The mask prior then has two tasks: preserve real observation geometry and produce varying contexts for the same sample.

A generative prior over observation masks

04 First learn where observations may occur, then learn the values there

The mask model sees only binary observation geometry. It learns the spatial distribution produced by clouds or orbital sampling, then helps the reconstruction model construct training tasks. It does not access the ground-truth field values to be filled in.

Modeling binary masks through continuous latent variables

The implementation uses a continuous latent-variable parameterization inspired by Bayesian Flow Networks (BFN). Each pixel has \(C=2\) classes: missing and observed. Let \(c\) denote the class and \(\boldsymbol e_c\) its one-hot vector. Embed with scale \(\kappa>0\), then add Gaussian noise:

\[ \boldsymbol z_0=\kappa\boldsymbol e_c,\qquad \boldsymbol z_t=\alpha_t\boldsymbol z_0+\sigma_t\boldsymbol\epsilon, \qquad \widehat{\boldsymbol e}_\eta \big(t,\operatorname{softmax}(\boldsymbol z_t)\big)\in\Delta^{C-1}. \]

Softmax acts along the class axis of each pixel. The network sees probability vectors and outputs predicted probabilities for clean classes; arbitrary spatial dependencies between pixels are allowed. Here \(\kappa\) distinguishes this scale from the earlier ensemble size. Below we retain the scaling notation \(K\equiv\kappa\) used in the mathematical statements.

Theorem 6 · Softmax factorization

Suppose \(h:\mathbb{R}^{C}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}\) satisfies, for every \(\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{C}\) and \(a\in\mathbb{R}\), \(h(\boldsymbol{x}+a\boldsymbol{1})=h(\boldsymbol{x})\). Define the relative interior of the probability simplex as

\[ \Delta^{C-1}_{\circ}=\operatorname{ri}(\Delta^{C-1}) =\left\{\boldsymbol{p}\in\mathbb{R}^{C}:p_j>0,\ \sum_{j=1}^{C}p_j=1\right\}. \]

Then there is a unique function \(g:\Delta^{C-1}_{\circ}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}\) such that, for every \(\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{C}\),

\[ h(\boldsymbol{x})=g(\operatorname{softmax}(\boldsymbol{x})), \qquad g(\boldsymbol{p})=h(\log\boldsymbol{p}). \]

The logarithm acts coordinatewise. Since \(\log\operatorname{softmax}(\boldsymbol{x})=\boldsymbol{x}-\log(\sum_je^{x_j})\boldsymbol{1}\), shift invariance gives existence, while \(\operatorname{softmax}(\log\boldsymbol{p})=\boldsymbol{p}\) gives uniqueness. For \(d\) pixels, if a constant can be independently added to each pixel's \(C\)-dimensional logits without changing \(h\), the result extends blockwise to a unique factorization through \((\Delta^{C-1}_{\circ})^d\).

Finite logits do not reach the simplex boundary, so the theorem does not specify boundary values. If a continuous extension to the closed simplex exists, it is unique; continuity of \(h\) alone does not guarantee its existence. For example, \(h(\boldsymbol{x})=x_1-x_2\) corresponds to \(g(\boldsymbol{p})=\log(p_1/p_2)\), which has no finite continuous extension to the entire boundary.

Theorem 7 · Shift invariance of the optimal data predictor

Let \(c\in\{1,\ldots,C\}\) have prior \(\pi_j=P(c=j)\), let \(K>0\), and set \(\boldsymbol{x}_0=K\boldsymbol{e}_c\). Fix time \(t\) and suppose

\[ \boldsymbol{x}_t=\alpha_tK\boldsymbol{e}_c+\sigma_t\boldsymbol{\epsilon}, \quad \boldsymbol{\epsilon}\sim\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{0},I_C), \quad \boldsymbol{\epsilon}\perp c,\quad \sigma_t>0. \]

Then both the class posterior and the optimal data predictor under squared loss, \(D_t^*(\boldsymbol{x})=\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{x}_0\mid\boldsymbol{x}_t=\boldsymbol{x}]\), are invariant under \(\boldsymbol{x}\mapsto\boldsymbol{x}+a\boldsymbol{1}\). Specifically,

\[ P(c=j\mid\boldsymbol{x}_t=\boldsymbol{x}) =\frac{\pi_j e^{\lambda_t x_j}}{\sum_{\ell=1}^{C}\pi_\ell e^{\lambda_t x_\ell}}, \quad \lambda_t=\frac{\alpha_tK}{\sigma_t^2}, \quad D_t^*(\boldsymbol{x})=K\big(P(c=j\mid\boldsymbol{x}_t=\boldsymbol{x})\big)_{j=1}^{C}. \]

For a complete mask, allow the pixel classes \(\boldsymbol{c}=(c_1,\ldots,c_d)\) to have any joint prior \(\pi(\boldsymbol{c})\). If the Gaussian noise on the stacked logits is independent, then

\[ P(\boldsymbol{c}\mid\boldsymbol{x}_t=\boldsymbol{x}) \propto\pi(\boldsymbol{c})\exp\left(\lambda_t\sum_{i=1}^{d}x_{i,c_i}\right). \]

Under independent shifts \(x_{i,j}\mapsto x_{i,j}+a_i\) for each pixel, every class configuration is multiplied by the same factor \(\exp(\lambda_t\sum_i a_i)\), which cancels upon normalization. Thus the joint posterior, per-pixel marginal posteriors, and posterior mean of the stacked target are all unchanged; mask pixels need not be independent. Together with Softmax factorization, this means the data predictor can receive only per-pixel Softmax probabilities. These input probabilities need not themselves equal the class posterior.

These two results answer a key architectural question: discarding the common shift of the logits loses no information needed by the optimal data predictor. The full score, however, includes a linear latent-variable term and cannot itself be treated as a shift-invariant function.

Theorem 8 · Exact reparameterization of discrete data matching and noise matching

Let \(\boldsymbol{x}_0=K\boldsymbol{e}_c\), \(K>0\), and \(\boldsymbol{x}_t=\alpha_t\boldsymbol{x}_0+\sigma_t\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\), with independent noise \(\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\sim\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{0},I_C)\). The following equalities apply at nondegenerate times with \(\alpha_t>0\) and \(\sigma_t>0\); endpoints require separate limits. Assume strictly positive time weights, finite expectations, and the same time-sampling distribution. If \(s_t^*=\nabla_{\boldsymbol{x}}\log p_t\), the population-optimal predictors under squared loss are

\[ \boldsymbol{\epsilon}_t^*(\boldsymbol{x}) =\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\mid\boldsymbol{x}_t=\boldsymbol{x}] =-\sigma_t\boldsymbol{s}_t^*(\boldsymbol{x}), \qquad D_t^*(\boldsymbol{x}) =\frac{\boldsymbol{x}+\sigma_t^2\boldsymbol{s}_t^*(\boldsymbol{x})}{\alpha_t}. \]

Define the class-probability network and its corresponding noise predictor as

\[ D_{\theta}(t,\boldsymbol{x})=K\widehat{\boldsymbol{e}}_{\theta}(t,\operatorname{softmax}(\boldsymbol{x})), \qquad \boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\theta}(t,\boldsymbol{x}) =\frac{\boldsymbol{x}-\alpha_tD_{\theta}(t,\boldsymbol{x})}{\sigma_t}. \]

Then, for every sample and every parameter setting,

\[ \boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\theta}(t,\boldsymbol{x}_t)-\boldsymbol{\epsilon} =\frac{\alpha_tK}{\sigma_t} \left(\boldsymbol{e}_c-\widehat{\boldsymbol{e}}_{\theta}(t,\operatorname{softmax}(\boldsymbol{x}_t))\right), \] \[ \begin{aligned} \mathcal{J}_{\mathrm{noise}}(\theta) &=\mathbb{E}\left[w_{\mathrm{NM}}(t)\|\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\theta}(t,\boldsymbol{x}_t)-\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\|^2\right]\\ &=\mathbb{E}\left[w_{\mathrm{NM}}(t)\frac{\alpha_t^2K^2}{\sigma_t^2} \|\widehat{\boldsymbol{e}}_{\theta}(t,\operatorname{softmax}(\boldsymbol{x}_t))-\boldsymbol{e}_c\|^2\right]. \end{aligned} \]

With data weight \(w(t)=w_{\mathrm{NM}}(t)\alpha_t^2K^2/\sigma_t^2\), the discrete data-matching loss and this noise-matching loss are exactly equal. Other strictly positive time weights have the same population optimum at each time for unrestricted predictors, but neither equal objectives nor identical optimal parameters are guaranteed for finite-capacity models. For complete masks, sum the equalities over all pixel-class coordinates.

\[ \boldsymbol{s}_{\theta}(t,\boldsymbol{x}) =-\frac{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\theta}(t,\boldsymbol{x})}{\sigma_t} =\frac{\alpha_tK\widehat{\boldsymbol{e}}_{\theta}(t,\operatorname{softmax}(\boldsymbol{x}))-\boldsymbol{x}}{\sigma_t^2}. \]

When the predicted class probabilities equal the true posterior, this expression equals the true score. The data predictor is shift invariant, whereas the full score and noise predictor satisfy, respectively,

\[ \boldsymbol{s}_t^*(\boldsymbol{x}+a\boldsymbol{1}) =\boldsymbol{s}_t^*(\boldsymbol{x})-\frac{a}{\sigma_t^2}\boldsymbol{1}, \qquad \boldsymbol{\epsilon}_t^*(\boldsymbol{x}+a\boldsymbol{1}) =\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_t^*(\boldsymbol{x})+\frac{a}{\sigma_t}\boldsymbol{1}. \]

Therefore, a network with only Softmax inputs represents the score indirectly through the data predictor and must retain the explicit linear latent-variable term. The exact reparameterization above applies to the defined Gaussian latent-variable model. Full equivalence to the original Bayesian Flow Network additionally requires matching the latent parameterization and noise schedule.

The reconstructed score can be used in the reverse probability-flow ODE. Generation starts from a Gaussian terminal approximation and ends by selecting the class with the largest logit at each pixel, giving a binary \(\widehat{\boldsymbol M}\). The exact mathematical relationships here apply to the specified Gaussian latent-variable model; equivalence to other BFN formulations requires matching latent variables and time schedules.

Observation-aligned generation

05 Realistic geometry must also overlap current observations to retain useful context

Unconditional generation may produce realistic masks with almost no intersection with the current sample. With too little context, there may be many queries, but the model loses the clues needed to infer spatial structure.

OAMP randomly retains some anchors from the currently valid observations, rather than forcing every observed location to remain in the context. Random anchors allow different partitions of the same sample.

\[ \begin{aligned} y_i&=\boldsymbol 1[r_i<\rho]M_i, &&r_i\sim\operatorname{Unif}(0,1),\quad 0<\rho<1,\\ \widehat e_i&=\left[\widehat{\boldsymbol e}_\eta (t,\operatorname{softmax}(\boldsymbol z_t))\right]_{i,2},\\ \mathcal L_{\mathrm{guide}}(\boldsymbol z_t,\boldsymbol y) &=-\frac1d\sum_{i=1}^d \left[y_i\log\widehat e_i+(1-y_i)\log(1-\widehat e_i)\right],\\ \boldsymbol z_{t_{j+1}} &\leftarrow\boldsymbol z_{t_{j+1}}^{\mathrm{base}} -w_g\nabla_{\boldsymbol z_{t_j}}\mathcal L_{\mathrm{guide}}. \end{aligned} \]

Guidance acts on the latent variables during reverse sampling, with \(w_g\) controlling its strength. The loss is normalized over the whole spatial domain, so variation in the number of observations per sample does not directly change the denominator. After generation, use \(\boldsymbol M_{\mathrm{ctx}}=\boldsymbol M\odot\widehat{\boldsymbol M}\) and \(\boldsymbol M_{\mathrm{qry}}=\boldsymbol M\odot(1-\widehat{\boldsymbol M})\). Anchors constrain generation; they are not new field measurements.

Theorem 9 · Marginal query positivity under capacity-guided partitioning

Assumption: complete coverage at a fixed intersection capacity.

Fix an observation mask \(\boldsymbol{M}\in\{0,1\}^d\), and write \(|\boldsymbol{M}|=\sum_iM_i\) for the number of valid observations. Let \(C_k\) denote the hard constraint satisfied by the generated mask:

\[ C_k:\quad |\widehat{\boldsymbol{M}}\odot\boldsymbol{M}|=k, \qquad 0<k<|\boldsymbol{M}|. \]

Write the support of the conditional generation distribution as

\[ S_{\mathrm{guided}}=\{\widehat{\boldsymbol{m}}\in\{0,1\}^d:P(\widehat{\boldsymbol{M}}=\widehat{\boldsymbol{m}}\mid C_k)>0\}. \]

Assume that for every coordinate with \(M_i=1\), there is at least one \(\widehat{\boldsymbol{M}}_a\in S_{\mathrm{guided}}\) such that \((\widehat{\boldsymbol{M}}_a)_i=0\). In other words, after limiting context capacity, no valid coordinate is permanently retained by every mask in the support.

Fix \(\boldsymbol{M}\in\{0,1\}^d\), let \(\widehat{\boldsymbol{M}}\sim P(\widehat{\boldsymbol{M}}\mid C_k)\) satisfy the hard intersection constraint above, and define

\[ \boldsymbol{M}_{\mathrm{ctx}}=\widehat{\boldsymbol{M}}\odot\boldsymbol{M}, \qquad \boldsymbol{M}_{\mathrm{qry}}=\boldsymbol{M}\odot(\boldsymbol{1}-\boldsymbol{M}_{\mathrm{ctx}}). \]

If complete coverage at a fixed intersection capacity holds, then for every valid observed coordinate with \(M_i=1\),

\[ P\big((\boldsymbol{M}_{\mathrm{qry}})_i=1\mid C_k\big)>0. \]

Because \(\boldsymbol{M}\) is binary, \(\boldsymbol{M}_{\mathrm{qry}}=\boldsymbol{M}\odot(\boldsymbol{1}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{M}})\). Thus, when \(M_i=1\),

\[ P\big((\boldsymbol{M}_{\mathrm{qry}})_i=1\mid C_k\big) =P(\widehat M_i=0\mid C_k) =\sum_{\substack{\widehat{\boldsymbol{m}}\in S_{\mathrm{guided}}\\\widehat m_i=0}} P(\widehat{\boldsymbol{M}}=\widehat{\boldsymbol{m}}\mid C_k) \ge P(\widehat{\boldsymbol{M}}=\widehat{\boldsymbol{M}}_a\mid C_k)>0. \]

Scope: The positivity condition here is conditional on fixed observations and the capacity event \(C_k\), describing marginal query opportunities across samples from the sampler. It is not equivalent to conditional query positivity given every context. Actual sampling uses random anchors and soft cross-entropy guidance, without fixing the intersection size exactly. The theorem therefore provides a sufficient condition at the support level; coverage of the actual sampler still requires experimental checks.

Mathematical coverage conditions and actual sampling behavior

The independent-intersection theorem conditions on the context; the capacity-guidance theorem conditions on fixed observations and a capacity event. They address different levels of coverage. The soft guidance used in practice does not exactly sample the hard-constrained distribution, so the theorems alone cannot establish positivity for every actual context.

What incomplete observations can identify

06 Why are structural priors still needed when conditional supervision is valid?

A model may learn effective predictions at supervised conditional states yet still be unable to uniquely determine an arbitrary complete joint distribution from all the fragments. These two questions concern different objects.

Theorem 10 · Unrestricted complete-field distributions are not generally identifiable from observations that are never complete

Vectorize the complete field within the valid domain as \(\boldsymbol{x}_0\in\mathbb R^d\). Let the binary observation mask \(\boldsymbol M\) have a fixed, known distribution \(q\), independent of \(\boldsymbol{x}_0\). If \(\Pr_q(\boldsymbol M=\boldsymbol 1_d)=0\), then within the unrestricted class of all probability distributions on \(\mathbb R^d\), the complete-field distribution cannot be identified from \((\boldsymbol M\odot\boldsymbol{x}_0,\boldsymbol M)\). Therefore, no recovery algorithm is consistent for every complete-field distribution in this class.

Specifically, define the observation operator

\[\mathcal T_q(P)=\operatorname{Law}_{P,q}(\boldsymbol M\odot\boldsymbol{x}_0,\boldsymbol M).\]

Identifiability requires that, for all \(P_1,P_2\) in the class, \(\mathcal T_q(P_1)=\mathcal T_q(P_2)\Rightarrow P_1=P_2\). Take \(\boldsymbol{x}_0\in\{-1,+1\}^d\) and consider the following two disjoint sets, each containing \(2^{d-1}\) elements. Define uniform distributions \(P_+,P_-\) on them:

\[\mathcal X_+=\left\{\boldsymbol x:\prod_{i=1}^d x_i=+1\right\},\qquad\mathcal X_-=\left\{\boldsymbol x:\prod_{i=1}^d x_i=-1\right\}.\]

For any proper subset \(S\subsetneq[d]\) and any sign assignment \(\boldsymbol x_S\), at least one remaining coordinate is unassigned. Under the fixed-product constraint, every sign except the last can be chosen freely, so each distribution has exactly \(2^{d-|S|-1}\) valid completions:

\[P_+(\boldsymbol{x}_{0,S}=\boldsymbol x_S)=P_-(\boldsymbol{x}_{0,S}=\boldsymbol x_S)=\frac{2^{d-|S|-1}}{2^{d-1}}=2^{-|S|}.\]

For any mask with \(q(m)>0\), \(S_m=\{i:m_i=1\}\subsetneq[d]\). Let \(\boldsymbol Y=\boldsymbol M\odot\boldsymbol{x}_0\). The conditional observation laws and their joint laws are then identical:

\[\begin{aligned}\operatorname{Law}_{P_+}(\boldsymbol Y\mid\boldsymbol M=m)&=\operatorname{Law}_{P_-}(\boldsymbol Y\mid\boldsymbol M=m),\\\Pr_{P_+,q}(\boldsymbol Y\in A,\boldsymbol M=m)&=q(m)\Pr_{P_+}(\boldsymbol Y\in A\mid\boldsymbol M=m)\\&=q(m)\Pr_{P_-}(\boldsymbol Y\in A\mid\boldsymbol M=m)\\&=\Pr_{P_-,q}(\boldsymbol Y\in A,\boldsymbol M=m).\end{aligned}\]

Thus \(\mathcal T_q(P_+)=\mathcal T_q(P_-)\), but \(P_+\ne P_-\). For any \(n\) independent and identically distributed observation pairs, an estimator \(\widehat P_n\) based on them has the same output distribution in these two worlds, with internal randomness coupled identically, and cannot converge to the two different true distributions respectively.

In two dimensions, let \(\boldsymbol M=(1,0)\) or \((0,1)\), each with probability \(1/2\) and independent of the field. Take

\[\begin{aligned}P_1((1,1))=P_1((-1,-1))&=\tfrac12,\\P_2((1,-1))=P_2((-1,1))&=\tfrac12.\end{aligned}\]

The two complete fields have opposite dependence structures but produce the same observation law. When only \(x_{0,1}\) is observed, the conditional means of the missing coordinate are, respectively,

\[\mathbb E_{P_1}[x_{0,2}\mid x_{0,1}]=x_{0,1},\qquad\mathbb E_{P_2}[x_{0,2}\mid x_{0,1}]=-x_{0,1}.\]

This shows that a lack of joint exposure creates predictive ambiguity. The result does not rule out identifiability under structural assumptions, nor does it invalidate supervised learning at states with positive query exposure. The mask-independence assumption is used only for this theorem; the real-ocean experiments still use the actual acquisition masks.

A two-dimensional example: identical marginals, opposite dependence

If at most one of two coordinates is observed at a time, the following distributions produce identical observations: either coordinate takes \(+1\) or \(-1\) with equal probability. Yet one distribution gives the coordinates the same sign and the other gives them opposite signs. Fragments alone cannot distinguish this full dependence structure.

This does not mean that physical fields cannot be reconstructed. Rather, universal recovery requires additional restrictions, such as identifiable structured distribution families, conditions on the observation mechanism, or effective domain priors. With finite data, sparse evidence can also make squared loss favor an average of multiple possible shapes, weakening fronts, elongated vortices, or local high- and low-value structures.

From distribution recovery to structured point reconstruction

The next goal is to learn better reconstruction rules: give recurring local relationships dedicated supervision, then let all observations correct the local estimates. Local priors provide an inductive bias; they do not remove non-identifiability for arbitrary distributions.

Estimate locally, correct globally

07 Learn structure from neighborhoods, then revise it with full-domain evidence

A field that is sparse overall may still contain local patches with relatively rich observations. A shared local model turns these patches into smaller learning tasks. Overlapping predictions form a reference field, and a global model then learns how to correct it.

A shared local predictor: different positions, shared parameters

The local model processes one patch at a time. It receives noisy observations and the observation mask within the patch, together with its absolute coordinates in the complete field, and predicts the field values inside it. All patches share one parameter set, allowing local relationships learned across different samples and neighborhoods to jointly support reconstruction. Coordinates tell the model where the patch is located, enabling it to adapt to statistical differences across ocean regions.

During training, one patch is selected uniformly from those containing query targets, and error is computed only on real observations temporarily hidden within it. Local variation thus receives a dedicated learning task. Cropping reorganizes existing supervision; it does not add independent observations or supply labels that were originally missing.

Assembling overlapping patches into a reference field

After training, the local model is frozen. Given the current context, it predicts all patches covering the full domain, including patches without query targets on that visit. A location may be predicted by multiple patches, which see different neighborhoods and may give different answers. Averaging the predictions that cover each location reconciles these overlapping estimates into a full-size reference field.

This reference field is the local field prior. It has the same spatial extent, channels, and units as the final target and provides an intermediate estimate conditioned on the current observations. The earlier mask prior answers where observations may occur; this field prior answers what a value may be given nearby observations.

A full-size assembly does not imply that all information has been used. Each patch predicts independently and sees only its own context. For a target region, consider all local patches covering it: observations outside these patches cannot affect its reference values. Overlap averaging reconciles local answers, while distant evidence must enter through the next stage.

Teaching the full-domain model to revise the reference field

The full-domain model sees all retained context together with the local reference field and predicts the residual to add to that field. It can adjust local judgments using observations outside the patches, as well as repair parts that the local model predicted poorly despite available neighborhood information:

\[ \underbrace{\text{final prediction}}_{\text{full-domain field estimate}} =\underbrace{\text{local prior}}_{\text{average of overlapping patches}} +\underbrace{\text{global residual}}_{\text{correction learned from all context}}. \]

The residual output head is initialized to zero, so the prediction at the start of full-domain training is exactly the local prior. The model then learns where, in which direction, and by how much to correct it, with any predicted location allowed to change. The local model remains frozen throughout; whenever the context changes, its corresponding prior is rebuilt.

Supervision still comes from real query values: at query locations, the temporarily hidden measurement minus the local prior tells the model the required correction. The local estimate need not already be the strongest standalone predictor. Its role is to provide a structured starting point that full-domain evidence can revise.

A single observation interface throughout

08 Connecting partitioning, local priors, and global correction into one training and reconstruction pipeline

The entire pipeline depends only on available observations. Mask construction determines the practice tasks, the local model builds a reference, and the global model learns corrections. The supplied real measurements are restored during inference.

Explicitly show the corrector where the reference field disagrees with observations

After supplying the reference field to the full-domain model, their relationship can be made easier to interpret. In addition to the noisy context, observation mask, and local prior, the corrector receives three intuitive auxiliary maps.

The overlap-count map records how many local patches predict each location, showing the model how the reference field was assembled. It describes construction geometry, rather than calibrated uncertainty.

The context-discrepancy map compares prior predictions with noisy measurements at supplied observation locations, showing where the reference field disagrees with current evidence. The boundary-discrepancy map focuses on non-context locations that are four-neighbors of observations. It compares the prior with the average of neighboring noisy observations, providing correction cues near observation boundaries. Discrepancies are absolute values averaged across channels.

All auxiliary maps are computed from the same actual noisy context, while query ground truth remains only on the loss side. These maps help the corrector use existing information. They are observable cues and cannot be treated as true error labels at missing locations.

From training to inference

  1. Construct practice tasks matching the observation mechanism. Keep each sample's original support fixed and construct context/query pairs using pixels, whole blocks, or real mask geometry. Black Sea CHL and Baltic Sea NANO use a frozen mask BFN; Global Ocean SSS uses a library of real track masks. After intersection, handle infeasible partitions or those with empty queries.
  2. Train the local model. Crop noisy context into patches, add absolute coordinates, and compute squared error only against query ground truth within each patch. Then freeze the local model.
  3. Build a reference and train the global residual. Evaluate every patch for the current context, average overlaps to form the prior, then compute discrepancy features. Update only the global corrector, which learns the true value minus the local prior at query locations.
  4. Reconstruct from the supplied observations. Construct the reference and residual at the same near-zero-noise time \(\delta\). Ocean experiments use all supplied observations by default; PDE experiments average \(K=8\) subcontexts by default. Each subcontext undergoes its own local construction and global correction before averaging.
  5. Preserve real measurements. Write the supplied observations back to the output and fill only missing locations within the valid domain.

All contexts for the same evaluation sample share a single near-zero-noise perturbation, and each context undergoes its own local prediction and full-domain correction. The default full-mask mode for ocean data evaluates once using all supplied observations. Here, all does not mean that a complete reference field is available; this mode also differs from randomly drawing just one subcontext.

At \(64\times 64\), CHL and NANO use nine overlapping \(32\times 32\) patches with stride 16; SSS uses \(16\times 16\) patches with stride 8, giving 49 patches, with scales selected on the validation set. The local model first builds the reference, the full-domain model then revises it, and real measurements are finally written back so that the output retains every supplied observation.

Does global correction actually use observations outside the neighborhood?

Fix a target block and remove only remote observations outside all relevant local patches. The local model receives the same inputs, so the reference field on the target block is unchanged. The actual global output shows a nonzero mean squared displacement on all three products, indicating that the corrector responds to evidence unavailable to local construction.

A response does not always imply a benefit. Further compare distance groups while deleting the same number of observations: Remote Mid and Remote Far deletions increase mean error for CHL and NANO, with 95% confidence intervals above zero. For SSS, Remote Near deletion increases error, Remote Far deletion decreases it, and the Remote Mid interval crosses zero. The role of remote evidence depends on the data product and distance range.

In input ablations, removing both discrepancy maps increases final MSE by 36.8%, 22.9%, and 17.4% on CHL, NANO, and SSS, respectively. This supports explicitly supplying the prior–observation relationship to a finite-capacity corrector. Since each variant is retrained, these numbers cannot be interpreted as the independent causal contribution of each feature.

From fragments to a revisable reconstruction

10 One research direction: making existing evidence more useful for learning and reconstruction

Learning from incomplete data first requires understanding which targets have supervision, then matching training tasks to real observations, and finally organizing how the model uses limited evidence.

  1. Effective conditional supervisionContext serves as input and queries determine the loss; coverage and selection conditions define the learned target.
  2. Observation-aligned practiceLearn mask geometry while balancing query opportunities and context information.
  3. Revisable structural priorsFirst learn local relationships, then let full-domain observations correct local estimates.

The context–query framework organizes supervision and sampling, while the mask prior further matches real observation geometry. Faced with identifiability limits for complete distributions, the local-to-global method instead splits reconstruction into first forming a reference and then learning a correction. Experiments show that local estimates can be useful intermediate representations revised by full-domain evidence, even when they are not the strongest standalone predictors.

Practical limits remain: query coverage cannot replace assumptions about the missingness mechanism, ensembling does not automatically eliminate bias, and deterministic priors add no new observations. Some block-missing reconstructions still have boundary seams. Truly missing ocean regions lack scorable ground truth, so current results mainly validate point-reconstruction quality on hidden measurements.
